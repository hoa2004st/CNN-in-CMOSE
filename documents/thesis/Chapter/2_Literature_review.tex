\section{Scope of Research}
\label{sec:scope}

This thesis is scoped to vision-based engagement recognition, which classifies a learner's engagement from a frontal webcam recording of one person. Within this scope the thesis concentrates on four parts, and this chapter is organised around them. These parts are the extraction of facial-behaviour and motion features, the modelling of the per-frame descriptors in time, the loss functions suited to imbalanced ordinal labels, and the metrics used to evaluate such models.

Several adjacent problems are left out of scope. Physiological sensors and interaction logs are not used, because they require instrumentation beyond a webcam. Facial-expression and emotion recognition are not treated as an end in themselves, and are referenced only where they inform how engagement features are designed. Finally, the thesis works with OpenFace and I3D features at the descriptor level rather than re-training the extractors: CMOSE ships these features precomputed, while for DAiSEE and the private set they are extracted from the raw video with the same toolkits (Section~\ref{sec:data}); how the extractors are trained internally is reviewed for understanding but not re-implemented.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Research on engagement recognition rests on a small number of datasets recorded in the wild, and two of them are used in this thesis. DAiSEE~\cite{daisee} is the most widely used. It provides short webcam clips annotated on a four-level ordinal engagement scale under uncontrolled conditions, which makes it realistic but noisy. CMOSE~\cite{cmose} is more recent and larger, and its expert annotation gives it cleaner labels, which is why this thesis takes it as the primary dataset and describes both datasets in full in Section~\ref{sec:data}. A systematic review of the field~\cite{review_engagement_cv} surveys the common pipelines and names two recurring open problems, severe class imbalance and subjective labels.

One family of methods first reduces each frame to a compact descriptor with a toolkit such as OpenFace~\cite{openface2}, and then models the resulting sequence in time. Representative pipelines feed OpenFace action units, gaze, and head pose to a recurrent network, as in an OpenFace$+$BiLSTM model~\cite{openface_bilstm}, or they apply dimensionality reduction and resampling before a convolutional or fully-connected classifier~\cite{openface_pca_smote}. Other work aggregates the features over time and compares classical learners such as support vector machines and gradient-boosted trees~\cite{classical_ml_engagement}. The advantage of this family is that the descriptors are compact, cheap to model, and interpretable, since each dimension names a specific behavioural quantity, and the reported results confirm that OpenFace descriptors carry a real engagement signal. The disadvantage is that the 709-dimensional descriptor is treated as one uniform vector and encoded by a single model. This descriptor mixes behavioural channels whose dynamics differ widely, so one temporal model is forced onto signals that do not share a timescale.

A second family learns directly from pixels. An EfficientNetV2~\cite{efficientnetv2} backbone followed by an LSTM~\cite{efficientnet_lstm_engagement} encodes appearance in each frame and integrates it over time, and context-aware affect-recognition models exploit spatial relationships in the wider scene~\cite{context_aware_emotion_3d}. The advantage of this family is that it captures appearance and motion cues that landmark-based descriptors discard, and it learns its features automatically rather than relying on a fixed toolkit. The disadvantage is that these models are heavier to train, require the raw video rather than precomputed features, and are harder to interpret, because the learned representation is not decomposed into nameable behavioural channels.

Considered together, the two families leave the same two limitations, and these limitations are the motivation for this thesis. The first limitation is that both families encode the facial features as a single block rather than by behavioural subsystem, even though the channels have very different timing, so the structure of the descriptor is discarded. The second limitation is shared with the wider field and concerns evaluation. Studies report accuracy in-domain and rarely test generalisation, yet on imbalanced data a high accuracy can hide a model that predicts the majority class and does not transfer to a new recording setting. This thesis is undertaken to address both limitations, by preserving the structure of the facial features through a feature-group model, and by evaluating every model on an imbalance-resistant ordinal metric across several datasets.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:feat}

\subsubsection{OpenFace 2.0 facial-behaviour descriptors}

OpenFace 2.0~\cite{openface2} is an open-source facial-behaviour analysis toolkit that processes a video frame by frame and, for each frame, estimates four kinds of measurement: the two- and three-dimensional facial landmarks that outline the face and eye regions, the head pose recovered from those landmarks, the eye-gaze direction vectors and angles for both eyes, and the intensity and presence of the facial action units of the Facial Action Coding System. Each frame is also tagged with a tracking-confidence and success flag that downstream pipelines use to discard poorly tracked frames.

In CMOSE the per-frame descriptor used here is 709-dimensional. By the meaning of their columns these numbers split into five feature groups, gaze (8 dimensions), eye landmarks (280), face landmarks (340), head pose (46), and action units (35). This decomposition is formalised in Section~\ref{sec:groups}, where the different temporal behaviour of the groups is used to motivate the proposed architecture in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}.

\subsubsection{I3D motion and appearance features}

The Inflated 3D ConvNet (I3D)~\cite{i3d} is a video representation obtained by inflating the two-dimensional convolutional filters of an ImageNet-pretrained image network into three dimensions, so they operate over space and time jointly, and then pretraining on the large Kinetics dataset for action recognition. I3D therefore captures appearance and short-range motion that landmark-based descriptors omit. In the data used here, CMOSE ships a single precomputed 1024-dimensional I3D embedding per clip, and for DAiSEE and the private set the same vector is extracted from the raw video. In every case it is one vector pooled over the whole clip, not a per-frame sequence, so the thesis treats I3D as a global clip-level stream of motion and appearance that complements the OpenFace descriptors.

\subsection{Temporal Architectures}
\label{sec:temporal}

A clip is a sequence of per-frame OpenFace descriptors, so a model must reduce a variable-length sequence to a single engagement prediction. Three architecture families dominate sequence modelling, and all three are used in this thesis, both as single-encoder baselines (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) and as the per-group encoders inside the proposed hybrid model (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). They differ in how they let one time step influence another.

\subsubsection{Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN)}

A Temporal Convolutional Network~\cite{tcn} applies one-dimensional convolutions along the time axis, and two design choices make it well suited to behavioural sequences. First, the convolutions are causal: the output at time $t$ depends only on inputs at times $\le t$, achieved by padding on the left and removing the surplus on the right. Second, successive layers use exponentially increasing dilation, so a layer at depth $l$ skips $2^{l}$ steps and a stack of $L$ layers reaches a receptive field that grows as $\mathcal{O}(2^{L})$ with few parameters. Each layer is a residual block (two dilated convolutions with weight normalisation, a rectified-linear nonlinearity, and dropout, summed with a possibly projected copy of the input) that stabilises deep stacks (Figure~\ref{fig:tcn_block}). The result is a strong inductive bias for short, local temporal motifs at multiple scales, which matches signals such as a head nod or an action-unit burst.

\subsubsection{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)}

The Long Short-Term Memory network~\cite{lstm} is a recurrent architecture that processes the sequence one step at a time while maintaining a cell state $c_t$ and a hidden state $h_t$ (Figure~\ref{fig:lstm_cell}). Three gates (input $i_t$, forget $f_t$, and output $o_t$) regulate how much new information is written, how much past information is retained, and how much of the cell state is exposed:
\begin{align}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f), &
i_t &= \sigma(W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i), \notag\\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o), &
\tilde{c}_t &= \tanh(W_c x_t + U_c h_{t-1} + b_c), \notag\\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t, &
h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t),
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ is the element-wise product. The gating lets the network carry information across many time steps without the vanishing-gradient problem of plain recurrent networks, giving it a bias toward longer-range dependencies. The cost is that the sequential recurrence is harder to parallelise than convolution or attention.

\subsubsection{Transformers}

The Transformer encoder~\cite{transformer} drops recurrence and convolution and instead relates all time steps directly through self-attention. For queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$ obtained by linear projections of the input, attention computes
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,
\end{equation}
so every output position is a learned weighted combination of every input position, with $d_k$ the key dimension. Because attention is permutation-invariant, order is injected through a positional encoding added to the input; this thesis uses the standard sinusoidal encoding. Multiple attention heads run in parallel and their outputs are concatenated, and each attention sub-layer is followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalisation throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer_encoder_layer}). The Transformer has the weakest temporal prior of the three families, since it can attend anywhere, which makes it flexible but more reliant on data.

\subsection{Loss Functions for Imbalance and Ordinality}
\label{sec:losses-lit}

Let a model produce logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ for the $C=4$ engagement classes. The softmax function maps these logits to a probability distribution over the classes, the predicted probability of class $c$ being
\begin{equation}
\widehat{y}_c = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_c = \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
so that $\widehat{y}_c\in(0,1)$ and $\sum_{c=1}^{C}\widehat{y}_c=1$. Let $y\in\{1,\dots,C\}$ be the ordinal ground-truth class, encoded as a one-hot vector through the indicator function $\mathbf{1}[\,\cdot\,]$, which returns $1$ when its argument is true and $0$ otherwise:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{1}[\,S\,] =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if the statement } S \text{ is true}\\
0 & \text{if } S \text{ is false}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:indicator}
\end{equation}
so that the one-hot component of the label is $y_c = \mathbf{1}[\,c = y\,]$. Three loss families are compared in this thesis; their exact application is given in Section~\ref{sec:losses-method}.

\textbf{Cross-entropy (CE)} is the standard classification loss,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} y_c \log \widehat{y}_c ,
\end{equation}
which, because $y$ is one-hot, reduces to the negative log-probability of the true class; it treats all classes and all errors as equally severe. On imbalanced data it is dominated by the majority class and on ordinal data it ignores class order, so it serves as the baseline against which the other losses are judged.

\textbf{Weighted cross-entropy} counteracts imbalance by scaling each class's contribution by a weight $w_c$ inversely proportional to its frequency,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{WCE}} = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c\, y_c \log \widehat{y}_c .
\end{equation}
This raises the cost of mistakes on rare classes such as E0 (Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage) and stops the model collapsing onto the majority, at the expense of some accuracy. Resampling methods such as SMOTE~\cite{smote} address the same imbalance at the data level; this thesis instead studies loss-level remedies, which require no change to the data pipeline.

\textbf{Ordinal (squared-EMD) loss}~\cite{squared_emd} encodes the order of the classes by comparing cumulative distributions. Writing the predicted cumulative distribution as $\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widehat{y}_j$ and that of the one-hot label as the unit step $\sum_{j=1}^{k} y_j$, the loss is the mean squared distance between the two step functions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{EMD}} = \frac{w_y}{C}\sum_{k=1}^{C}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widehat{y}_j-\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_j\right)^2 ,
\label{eq:emd}
\end{equation}
the squared Earth Mover's Distance (also called the CDF-MSE) between the predicted and target distributions over the ordered classes~\cite{squared_emd}, not the unsquared 1-Wasserstein distance; the leading $w_y$ is the weight of the true class (Section~\ref{sec:losses-method}), set to $1$ in the unweighted variant. Because moving probability mass across several classes costs more than moving it to an adjacent class, this loss directly penalises the large ordinal errors (E0$\leftrightarrow$E3) that accuracy and plain cross-entropy treat as~ordinary.

All models are optimised with Adam~\cite{adam}, an adaptive first-order optimiser that maintains per-parameter running estimates of the first and second moments of the~gradient.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics-lit}

Because the labels are ordinal and imbalanced, the choice of metric is itself a research question. This thesis reports six metrics over a test set of $N$ samples with true labels $y_i$ and predicted labels $\widehat{y}_i$, but treats exactly one, QWK, as the primary metric that selects the best model; the other five are secondary, reported to describe a result but never to choose one. Where a confusion matrix is needed, $O_{ij}$ denotes the number of samples of true class $i$ predicted as class $j$.

\textbf{Accuracy} is the fraction of correct predictions,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,y_i=\widehat{y}_i\,\right].
\end{equation}
It is reported for comparability with the literature but is not a primary metric here, because on imbalanced data it rewards majority prediction.

\textbf{Macro-accuracy} (balanced accuracy) is the mean of the per-class recalls,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MacroAcc} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,y_i=\widehat{y}_i=c\,\right]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,y_i=c\,\right]},
\end{equation}
giving every class equal weight regardless of frequency. It exposes the minority-class collapse that accuracy hides; here it is a secondary metric, reported to characterise class balance but never used to select a model.

\textbf{Mean absolute error (MAE)} treats the class indices as an ordinal scale and averages the distance between truth and prediction,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|y_i-\widehat{y}_i\right|,
\end{equation}
while macro-MAE averages the per-class MAE so errors on rare classes are not drowned out,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MacroMAE} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,y_i=c\,\right]\left|y_i-\widehat{y}_i\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,y_i=c\,\right]}.
\end{equation}
Both reward predictions that are close on the scale even when not exactly right, and both are smaller-is-better. Macro-MAE is the natural ordinal-distance companion to QWK, with each class weighted equally so the rare disengaged classes are not masked; like the other secondary metrics it is reported alongside QWK and never used to select a model.

\textbf{Cohen's kappa}~\cite{cohenkappa} measures agreement corrected for chance. With observed agreement $p_o$ and expected agreement $p_e$, $\kappa=(p_o-p_e)/(1-p_e)$. In its weighted form it is
\begin{equation}
\kappa = 1 - \frac{\sum_{ij} w_{ij}\,O_{ij}}{\sum_{ij} w_{ij}\,E_{ij}},
\end{equation}
where $E_{ij}$ is the expected count under independent marginals and $w_{ij}$ is a penalty matrix. The unweighted variant uses $w_{ij}=1$ off the diagonal and $0$ on it.

\textbf{Quadratic-weighted kappa (QWK)} is the same coefficient with the quadratic penalty
\begin{equation}
w_{ij} = \frac{(i-j)^2}{(C-1)^2},
\label{eq:qwk}
\end{equation}
so disagreements are penalised in proportion to the square of the ordinal distance between the true and predicted classes. QWK suits this problem because it is corrected for chance, respects class order, and cannot be inflated by majority prediction. It is the thesis's primary metric and the only criterion used to select the best model, with macro-accuracy and macro-MAE reported as secondary companions that describe a result but never choose it.

\subsection{Statistical Measures for Result Analysis}
\label{sec:stats-lit}

Beyond scoring each model, the results chapters ask three comparative questions that need their own tools: do the six metrics rank the configurations the same way (Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}), does in-domain strength predict cross-dataset strength (Section~\ref{sec:cross-dataset}), and do different models make the same per-clip decisions (Section~\ref{sec:agreement})? The first two are rank-correlation questions; the third is an agreement question.

\textbf{Spearman's rank correlation} $\rho$~\cite{spearman} measures how well a monotonic relationship describes two variables by applying the Pearson correlation to their ranks. For $n$ paired observations with rank differences $d_i$ and no ties it reduces to
\begin{equation}
\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i^{2}}{n\,(n^{2}-1)},
\end{equation}
ranging from $-1$ (perfectly inverted) through $0$ (no monotonic relation) to $+1$ (identical ordering). This thesis uses $\rho$ to test whether a model's in-domain QWK predicts its QWK on unseen target datasets.

\textbf{Kendall's $\tau$}~\cite{kendall} measures rank agreement through concordance. Counting the pairs ordered the same way in both variables (concordant, $n_c$) against those ordered oppositely (discordant, $n_d$), with no ties,
\begin{equation}
\tau_a = \frac{n_c - n_d}{\binom{n}{2}},
\end{equation}
again bounded by $[-1,1]$. It is less sensitive to small samples and to a few large rank displacements than $\rho$, so it is used here to quantify how far the six metrics agree on the ranking of the baseline configurations.

Prediction agreement asks whether two models agree clip by clip rather than merely score alike, measured by Cohen's $\kappa$ (Section~\ref{sec:metrics-lit}) between the two models' predicted-label vectors; chance-correction matters because two models that lean toward the majority class agree often by default. The headroom from combining a set of $M$ configurations is bounded by the oracle accuracy, the fraction of clips at least one configuration classifies correctly,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{OracleAcc} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\exists\, m\in\{1,\dots,M\}:\ \widehat{y}^{(m)}_n = y_n\,\right],
\end{equation}
which upper-bounds any fixed combiner and is contrasted with the realistic majority-vote accuracy. A large gap between the best single model, the majority vote, and the oracle indicates that the models err on different clips and that an ensemble has room to improve.
