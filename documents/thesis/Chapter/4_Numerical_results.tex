\section{Overview}
\label{sec:results-overview}

This chapter reports the experiments defined in Chapter~3 and answers the four
research questions of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}. Section~\ref{sec:dataset-analysis}
characterises the datasets and their imbalance. Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}
establishes baseline and loss-function behaviour in-domain and justifies why the
architecture study is anchored on CMOSE. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} is the core
semantic-group hybrid ablation (RQ1--RQ3). Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} is the
cross-dataset generalisation study, and Section~\ref{sec:private} --- the
practically most important result --- evaluates generalisation to the
self-collected private set (RQ4). Section~\ref{sec:error} is an error analysis,
and Section~\ref{sec:results-summary} summarises. Unless stated otherwise,
``in-domain'' means trained and tested on the same corpus, and the primary metrics
are \textbf{QWK} and \textbf{macro-accuracy}. Each section ends with an explicit
conclusion.

\section{Dataset Analysis}
\label{sec:dataset-analysis}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{dataset_class_distribution_overall.png}
\caption{Engagement class distribution by dataset.}
\label{fig:dataset_class_distribution_overall}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:T1_dataset_stats} and
Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_class_distribution_overall} show that all three corpora
are heavily imbalanced toward the upper-middle classes, and in
\emph{different directions}. CMOSE is 69.4\% \emph{Engage} with only 2.8\%
\emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage}, whereas DAiSEE is 50.3\% \emph{Engage} and
43.8\% \emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Engage} with just 0.7\%
\emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage}. The rare disengaged classes are precisely the
ones a useful monitoring system must catch, yet they are a fraction of a percent
to a few percent of the data. The split-wise distribution
(Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_class_distribution_by_split}) confirms that this
imbalance is consistent across the train, validation, and test partitions, so it
is a property of the problem rather than of a particular split.
\input{Table/T1_dataset_stats}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{dataset_class_distribution_by_split.png}
\caption{Class distribution by split, per dataset.}
\label{fig:dataset_class_distribution_by_split}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} The data are severely and \emph{heterogeneously} imbalanced.
This already predicts difficulty when transferring between corpora
(Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}), justifies the imbalance-aware losses
(Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}), and confirms that accuracy alone cannot be
trusted as a metric, since predicting the majority class would score well on
every corpus.

\section{Base Models and Loss Functions (In-Domain)}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{base_models_indomain.png}
\caption{Base models $\times$ losses, in-domain on CMOSE.}
\label{fig:base_models_indomain}
\end{figure}

Among the five monolithic baselines (Table~\ref{tab:T2_base_indomain}), the
\textbf{OpenFace TCN with cross-entropy} is the strongest on ordinal agreement:
\textbf{QWK 0.537}, accuracy 0.761. The I3D MLP attains the highest \emph{raw
accuracy} (0.773) but a lower QWK (0.519) and macro-accuracy (0.526) under
cross-entropy --- it wins accuracy by leaning on the majority class. The TCN's
local temporal bias edges out the LSTM, the Transformer, and the flat MLP. This
QWK of \textbf{0.537 is the bar the hybrid must clear} in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.
\input{Table/T2_base_indomain}

\textbf{The loss trade-off.} Figure~\ref{fig:loss_accuracy_tradeoff} makes the
accuracy-versus-balance tension explicit. Cross-entropy points sit in the
lower-right (high accuracy, low macro-accuracy); weighted-CE and ordinal points
move up and to the left (more balanced, less accurate). For the I3D MLP, for
instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss moves the operating
point from (accuracy 0.773, macro-accuracy 0.526) to (0.640, 0.635). There is no
universally best loss; the choice is a Pareto trade-off, and for engagement
monitoring --- where missing disengagement is costly --- the balanced losses are
defensible despite their lower headline accuracy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{loss_accuracy_tradeoff.png}
\caption{Accuracy versus macro-accuracy trade-off by loss.}
\label{fig:loss_accuracy_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce} shows representative training and validation
loss curves; all baselines converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch
cap, so the differences above are architectural rather than optimisation
artefacts.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{loss_curves_cmose_ce.png}
\caption{Representative training/validation loss curves (CMOSE, cross-entropy).}
\label{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why CMOSE for the in-domain analysis?} The in-domain comparison above and
the architecture ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} are conducted on CMOSE
rather than DAiSEE for a principled reason: DAiSEE carries far weaker in-domain
ordinal signal. Table~\ref{tab:T7_indomain_datasets} and
Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee} show that the best in-domain model
reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535
versus 0.289). On DAiSEE the best models barely exceed chance-level ordinal
agreement, so architecture differences there would be dominated by label noise
rather than model quality. CMOSE is the only corpus on which architecture choices
can be compared meaningfully.
\input{Table/T7_indomain_datasets}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{indomain_cmose_vs_daisee.png}
\caption{In-domain ordinal signal: CMOSE versus DAiSEE.}
\label{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} The OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy is the strongest
baseline (QWK 0.537) and sets the bar for the hybrid; loss choice is a genuine
Pareto trade-off between accuracy and balance with no universal winner; and CMOSE,
not DAiSEE, is the right corpus on which to develop and compare architectures.

\section{Semantic-Group Hybrid Ablation (Core Result)}
\label{sec:ablation}

\textbf{Does the hybrid beat the baseline, and how robustly?}
Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_ablation_distribution} plots the QWK of all 243 per-group
encoder configurations, split by whether the I3D stream is added, against the
best-baseline line (0.537).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{hybrid_ablation_distribution.png}
\caption{Hybrid ablation: QWK across all 243 per-group encoder configurations,
with and without I3D, against the best baseline.}
\label{fig:hybrid_ablation_distribution}
\end{figure}

The \textbf{I3D-fused hybrid family is centred above the baseline} (median
$\approx0.553$; best 0.605), whereas the \textbf{OpenFace-only hybrid straddles
it} (median $\approx0.522$; best 0.584). The important message is the robustness
of the \emph{family}, not of any single cell: \textbf{83\% of the 243 I3D-fused
configurations clear the baseline}, against 27\% of the OpenFace-only ones, so the
gain comes from the architecture family rather than from one lucky configuration
(\textbf{RQ1, RQ3}). Table~\ref{tab:T4_hybrid_topk} lists the top-10
configurations; the best overall is the I3D hybrid \texttt{TCN\_T\_TCN\_LSTM\_T}
(QWK 0.605, accuracy 0.784), and every top configuration mixes at least two
encoder types.
\input{Table/T4_hybrid_topk}

\textbf{Which group's encoder actually matters?}
Table~\ref{tab:T5_group_marginal} and Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_group_marginal} show
the mean QWK when each group uses a TCN, a Transformer, or an LSTM, marginalising
over all other groups.
\input{Table/T5_group_marginal}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{hybrid_group_marginal.png}
\caption{Per-group marginal effect of the encoder choice on in-domain QWK.}
\label{fig:hybrid_group_marginal}
\end{figure}

Only \textbf{head pose} shows a clear preference --- TCN (0.545), ahead of LSTM
(0.541) and Transformer (0.523), a spread of 0.022. The other four groups are
essentially encoder-agnostic (spread $\le0.006$). This is an interpretable design
rule: head dynamics such as nodding and turning away are temporally local and
convolution-friendly, while the gaze, landmark, and action-unit streams are
insensitive to the encoder choice (\textbf{RQ2}). It also honestly bounds the
contribution: most of the 243-configuration grid is flat.

\textbf{Best hybrid versus best baseline.}
Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_best_comparison} summarises the headline comparison: the
best I3D-fused hybrid improves on the best baseline by \textbf{+0.068 QWK} (0.605
versus 0.537), and also improves accuracy (0.784 versus 0.761). The gain is
real but \textbf{modest}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{hybrid_best_comparison.png}
\caption{Best hybrid versus best base model, in-domain on CMOSE.}
\label{fig:hybrid_best_comparison}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} Decomposing the OpenFace descriptor and encoding each group
separately helps (RQ1): as a \emph{family}, the I3D-fused hybrid is centred above
the best monolithic baseline, with the majority of its configurations clearing the
bar and the best reaching QWK 0.605. Adding the I3D stream is beneficial and makes
the gain robust (RQ3). Only head pose has a clear encoder preference --- a TCN
(RQ2) --- so the contribution is a sound, interpretable framework with an
incremental headline gain, not a large jump in state of the art.

\section{Cross-Dataset Generalization}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{crossdomain_base.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best base model per cell.}
\label{fig:crossdomain_base}
\end{figure}

The 3$\times$3 heatmaps (Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} and
Table~\ref{tab:T3_crossdomain}) are the second headline result.
\textbf{On the diagonal} (train and test on the same corpus) QWK is healthy
(CMOSE 0.54). \textbf{Off the diagonal it collapses}:
CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE QWK $=0.02$ and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE $=0.05$ ---
essentially chance-level ordinal agreement. Crucially, \textbf{raw accuracy hides
this}: CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE accuracy is a deceptively reasonable 0.49 and
DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.69, purely because predicting the majority class scores
well on imbalanced targets. This is direct evidence that \textbf{accuracy is the
wrong metric} for this problem and that engagement models are \textbf{not portable
off-the-shelf}.
\input{Table/T3_crossdomain}

The most actionable cell is the unseen \textbf{Private} column: training on the
\textbf{Combined} corpus gives the best private-set QWK (0.285), beating
DAiSEE-only (0.256) and CMOSE-only (0.190). Pooling source datasets is a simple,
effective mitigation for an unknown target distribution (\textbf{RQ4}). The hybrid
heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_hybrid}) shows the same pattern, confirming
the gap is a property of the problem, not of a particular architecture.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{crossdomain_hybrid.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best hybrid per cell.}
\label{fig:crossdomain_hybrid}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} Engagement classifiers do not generalise across datasets:
off-diagonal QWK falls to near zero even when accuracy looks acceptable, so
ordinal agreement --- not accuracy --- must be reported on imbalanced engagement
data. Among the available levers, \emph{pooling source corpora} is the most
effective simple remedy for an unknown target (RQ4).

\section{Private-Set Generalization: The Real-World Test}
\label{sec:private}

The most consequential evaluation in this thesis is on the \textbf{private set} ---
the 366 clips we collected and hand-labelled ourselves
(Section~\ref{sec:private-collection}), processed through the identical
OpenFace/I3D pipeline and used \textbf{for testing only}. Because no model is ever
trained on it, the private set is a genuine ``in-the-wild'' probe of deployment:
it has its own recording conditions and its own label prior (58\% Engage, 31\%
Highly-Engage, only 2.7\% Highly-Disengage). For this set there is no in-domain
option at all; the \emph{only} design lever is the choice of training source,
which makes it the cleanest test of the two contributions of this thesis acting
together.

Figure~\ref{fig:private_by_source} and Table~\ref{tab:T6_private_by_source} report
the best base model and the best semantic-group hybrid on the private set, for
each training source.
\input{Table/T6_private_by_source}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{private_by_source.png}
\caption{Private-set generalisation by training source: best base versus best
hybrid.}
\label{fig:private_by_source}
\end{figure}

Two effects compound, and both favour this thesis's contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Training source matters, and the combined corpus wins.} For both
  model families, training on \textbf{Combined} generalises best to the private
  set; a single source is markedly worse (best base QWK: Combined 0.285 versus
  DAiSEE 0.256 versus CMOSE 0.190). Pooling heterogeneous source corpora is the
  most effective simple route to an unseen target.
  \item \textbf{The hybrid helps more here than in-domain.} The semantic-group
  hybrid beats the best base model for \emph{every} training source, and the gap is
  \textbf{largest exactly where it matters}: under combined training the hybrid
  reaches \textbf{QWK 0.379 versus 0.285} for the best base, a \textbf{$+0.094$}
  improvement --- wider than the $+0.068$ in-domain CMOSE gain
  (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). The overall best private-set model is the
  combined-trained I3D-fused hybrid \texttt{T\_T\_T\_LSTM\_T} (QWK 0.379,
  accuracy 0.607).
\end{enumerate}

In other words, the architecture contribution and the combined-training insight
are not independent curiosities: they \textbf{stack}, and they stack most strongly
on the hardest, most realistic, self-collected target.

Figure~\ref{fig:private_confusion_combined} shows the row-normalised confusion of
this headline model on the private set. The ordinal structure is captured (mass
concentrates on the diagonal and its neighbours): \emph{Engage} recall is 0.72 and
\emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Engage} 0.53, with errors mostly to adjacent classes. As
in-domain, the rare disengaged classes are the failure mode under shift ---
\emph{Disengage} recall is only 0.29 and \textbf{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage
collapses to 0.00} (all ten private HD clips are misread as DE/EG).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{private_confusion_combined.png}
\caption{Private-set confusion, combined-trained I3D hybrid
\texttt{T\_T\_T\_LSTM\_T} (QWK 0.379), row-normalised.}
\label{fig:private_confusion_combined}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} On the decisive, test-only private set the two contributions
\emph{compound}: combined-corpus training with the semantic-group hybrid is the
best model (QWK 0.379), beating the best baseline by a wider margin than in-domain
($+0.094$ versus $+0.068$). The practical payoff of the thesis is therefore
concentrated exactly where it matters most --- on unseen, real-world data ---
while recognising rare disengagement under distribution shift remains the central
open problem.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{confusion_best_models.png}
\caption{Confusion matrices of the best base versus the best hybrid (in-domain,
row-normalised).}
\label{fig:confusion_best_models}
\end{figure}

The row-normalised confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_best_models}) and
the per-class F1 scores (Figure~\ref{fig:per_class_f1}) localise where the hybrid
helps and where the problem remains open. Relative to the best baseline, the
hybrid improves recall on the ordinal extreme \textbf{Highly\nobreakdash-Engage}
($0.38\to0.51$) and on \textbf{Disengage} ($0.45\to0.55$), while keeping
\emph{Engage} near 0.90 ($0.91\to0.90$). However, the rarest class
\textbf{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage} stays unsolved and is slightly traded away (base
recall $0.40\to$ hybrid 0.37): the model reallocates capacity toward the more
frequent extreme.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{per_class_f1.png}
\caption{Per-class F1: best base versus best hybrid.}
\label{fig:per_class_f1}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.} The hybrid's improvement is real but \textbf{uneven}: it
strengthens the well-populated upper-engagement region and the middle disengaged
class, but the $\sim$2--3\%-frequency
\emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage} class remains the principal open challenge,
both in-domain and --- more severely --- under distribution shift.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:results-summary}

This chapter answered the four research questions. The datasets are severely and
heterogeneously imbalanced (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-analysis}); the best
monolithic baseline is the OpenFace TCN at QWK 0.537, and loss choice is a Pareto
trade-off (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}). The semantic-group hybrid, as a
family, exceeds that baseline and is best when fused with I3D (QWK 0.605), with
head pose the only group preferring a specific encoder (a TCN)
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, RQ1--RQ3). Engagement models do not transfer across
corpora --- off-diagonal QWK collapses while accuracy hides it --- and pooling
corpora mitigates this best (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}, RQ4). On the decisive
self-collected private set the architecture and the combined-training insight
compound to the best result (QWK 0.379, $+0.094$ over the baseline)
(Section~\ref{sec:private}). The error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:error}) shows
the gains are concentrated in the upper-engagement region, with
Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage the remaining open problem. Chapter~5 draws these
findings together and outlines future work.
