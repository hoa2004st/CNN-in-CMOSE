\section{Overview}
\label{sec:results-overview}

This chapter develops the proposed model \emph{in-domain} --- trained and tested on the same corpus --- and in doing so fixes the two methodological choices that the rest of the thesis depends on.
It proceeds as a single argument rather than a list of independent experiments.
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} first compares the five monolithic baselines under the three losses; this comparison is what \textbf{selects the training loss (cross-entropy) and the primary metrics (QWK and macro-accuracy)} used everywhere afterwards, establishes the baseline QWK that the hybrid must beat, and explains why the architecture study is anchored on CMOSE.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} then runs the core semantic-group hybrid ablation under exactly those choices, answering RQ1--RQ3.
Section~\ref{sec:error} localises, class by class, where the in-domain gain comes from and where the problem remains open.
Chapter~5 takes the resulting best model out of domain.
Throughout, the class imbalance characterised in Section~\ref{sec:data} is the backdrop: it is the reason accuracy is reported but never trusted, and the reason the rare disengaged classes recur as the failure mode.
Each section ends with an explicit conclusion that sets up the next.

\section{Base Models, Losses, and Metrics (In-Domain)}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

This section has a single purpose: before building anything, settle \emph{what to optimise}, \emph{how to measure it}, and \emph{what bar a new architecture must clear}.
Those three decisions --- the training loss, the primary metrics, and the baseline QWK --- are read off the comparison of the five monolithic baselines under the three losses, and they govern every later experiment.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{base_models_indomain.png}
\caption{Base models $\times$ losses, in-domain on CMOSE.}
\label{fig:base_models_indomain}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The strongest baseline, and the bar.}
Among the five monolithic baselines (Table~\ref{tab:T2_base_indomain}), the \textbf{OpenFace TCN with cross-entropy} is the strongest on ordinal agreement: \textbf{QWK 0.537}, accuracy 0.761.
The I3D MLP attains the highest \emph{raw accuracy} (0.773) but a lower QWK (0.519) and macro-accuracy (0.526) under cross-entropy --- it wins accuracy by leaning on the majority class, exactly the failure mode the imbalance of Section~\ref{sec:data} predicts.
The TCN's local temporal bias edges out the LSTM, the Transformer, and the flat MLP.
This QWK of \textbf{0.537 is the bar the hybrid must clear} in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.
\input{Table/T2_base_indomain}

\textbf{The loss is a Pareto trade-off.}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_accuracy_tradeoff} makes the accuracy-versus-balance tension explicit.
Cross-entropy points sit in the lower-right (high accuracy, low macro-accuracy); weighted-CE and ordinal points move up and to the left (more balanced, less accurate).
For the I3D MLP, for instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss moves the operating point from (accuracy 0.773, macro-accuracy 0.526) to (0.640, 0.635).
There is no universally best loss: the choice is a genuine Pareto trade-off, and for engagement monitoring --- where missing disengagement is costly --- the balanced losses are defensible despite their lower headline accuracy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{loss_accuracy_tradeoff.png}
\caption{Accuracy versus macro-accuracy trade-off by loss.}
\label{fig:loss_accuracy_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Decision 1 --- the primary metrics.}
The trade-off above shows directly why accuracy cannot be the yardstick: a model can buy accuracy by collapsing onto \emph{Engage}, and on these imbalanced labels (Section~\ref{sec:data}) that collapse is rewarded rather than penalised.
The remainder of the thesis therefore reports \textbf{QWK and macro-accuracy as the two primary metrics} --- QWK because it is corrected for chance, respects the class order, and cannot be inflated by majority prediction, and macro-accuracy because it exposes minority-class recall directly --- with the ordinal \textbf{MAE} retained as a secondary distance measure and raw accuracy reported only for comparability with the literature.

\textbf{Decision 2 --- the training loss for the architecture study.}
The strongest ordinal agreement of the whole baseline grid is obtained under \textbf{cross-entropy} (the bar-setting OpenFace TCN at QWK 0.537); the balanced losses trade QWK and accuracy for minority recall rather than improving the primary metric.
Cross-entropy is therefore the natural loss on which to develop the architecture: it maximises the primary metric and isolates the effect of the \emph{architecture} from the confound of the loss.
The semantic-group hybrid ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} is consequently trained with cross-entropy, and the balanced losses are revisited where they matter most --- recovering rare-class recall (Section~\ref{sec:error}) and surviving distribution shift (Chapter~5).
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce} shows representative training and validation loss curves; all baselines converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch cap, so the differences above are architectural rather than optimisation artefacts.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{loss_curves_cmose_ce.png}
\caption{Representative training/validation loss curves (CMOSE, cross-entropy).}
\label{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Decision 3 --- why CMOSE for the architecture study.}
The comparison above and the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} are conducted on CMOSE rather than DAiSEE for a principled reason: DAiSEE carries far weaker in-domain ordinal signal.
Table~\ref{tab:T7_indomain_datasets} and Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee} show that the best in-domain model reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535 versus 0.289).
On DAiSEE the best models barely exceed chance-level ordinal agreement, so architecture differences there would be dominated by label noise rather than model quality.
CMOSE is the only corpus on which architecture choices can be compared meaningfully; DAiSEE re-enters the study in Chapter~5, where it is a \emph{transfer} source and target rather than a development corpus.
\input{Table/T7_indomain_datasets}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{indomain_cmose_vs_daisee.png}
\caption{In-domain ordinal signal: CMOSE versus DAiSEE.}
\label{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
This section fixed the experimental frame for the rest of the thesis.
The strongest baseline is the OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy at \textbf{QWK 0.537}, which is the bar to beat.
Because loss choice is a Pareto trade-off in which cross-entropy gives the best ordinal agreement, the architecture study is trained with \textbf{cross-entropy} and judged by \textbf{QWK and macro-accuracy} (with MAE secondary), on \textbf{CMOSE}, the only corpus with enough in-domain signal to separate architectures.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} now applies exactly this frame.

\section{Semantic-Group Hybrid Ablation (Core Result)}
\label{sec:ablation}

Trained with cross-entropy and judged by QWK and macro-accuracy --- the choices fixed in Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} --- the semantic-group hybrid is now compared against the baseline bar of 0.537.

\textbf{Does the hybrid beat the baseline, and how robustly?}
Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_ablation_distribution} plots the QWK of all 243 per-group encoder configurations, split by whether the I3D stream is added, against the best-baseline line (0.537).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{hybrid_ablation_distribution.png}
\caption{Hybrid ablation: QWK across all 243 per-group encoder configurations,
with and without I3D, against the best baseline.}
\label{fig:hybrid_ablation_distribution}
\end{figure}

The \textbf{I3D-fused hybrid family is centred above the baseline} (median $\approx0.553$; best 0.605), whereas the \textbf{OpenFace-only hybrid straddles it} (median $\approx0.522$; best 0.584).
The important message is the robustness of the \emph{family}, not of any single cell: \textbf{83\% of the 243 I3D-fused configurations clear the baseline}, against 27\% of the OpenFace-only ones, so the gain comes from the architecture family rather than from one lucky configuration (\textbf{RQ1, RQ3}).
Table~\ref{tab:T4_hybrid_topk} lists the top-10 configurations; the best overall is the I3D hybrid \texttt{TCN\_T\_TCN\_LSTM\_T} (QWK 0.605, accuracy 0.784), and every top configuration mixes at least two encoder types.
\input{Table/T4_hybrid_topk}

\textbf{Which group's encoder actually matters?}
Table~\ref{tab:T5_group_marginal} and Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_group_marginal} show the mean QWK when each group uses a TCN, a Transformer, or an LSTM, marginalising over all other groups.
\input{Table/T5_group_marginal}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{hybrid_group_marginal.png}
\caption{Per-group marginal effect of the encoder choice on in-domain QWK.}
\label{fig:hybrid_group_marginal}
\end{figure}

Only \textbf{head pose} shows a clear preference --- TCN (0.545), ahead of LSTM (0.541) and Transformer (0.523), a spread of 0.022.
The other four groups are essentially encoder-agnostic (spread $\le0.006$).
This is an interpretable design rule: head dynamics such as nodding and turning away are temporally local and convolution-friendly, while the gaze, landmark, and action-unit streams are insensitive to the encoder choice (\textbf{RQ2}).
It also honestly bounds the contribution: most of the 243-configuration grid is flat.

\textbf{Best hybrid versus best baseline.}
Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_best_comparison} summarises the headline comparison: the best I3D-fused hybrid improves on the best baseline by \textbf{+0.068 QWK} (0.605 versus 0.537), and also improves accuracy (0.784 versus 0.761).
The gain is real but \textbf{modest}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{hybrid_best_comparison.png}
\caption{Best hybrid versus best base model, in-domain on CMOSE.}
\label{fig:hybrid_best_comparison}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Decomposing the OpenFace descriptor and encoding each group separately helps (RQ1): as a \emph{family}, the I3D-fused hybrid is centred above the best monolithic baseline, with the majority of its configurations clearing the bar and the best reaching QWK 0.605.
Adding the I3D stream is beneficial and makes the gain robust (RQ3).
Only head pose has a clear encoder preference --- a TCN (RQ2) --- so the contribution is a sound, interpretable framework with an incremental headline gain, not a large jump in state of the art.
The next section asks \emph{which classes} this gain comes from; Chapter~5 asks whether it survives outside the training corpus.

\section{In-Domain Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error}

The headline QWK gain of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} is an average; this section opens it up class by class to see where the hybrid actually helps in-domain.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{confusion_best_models.png}
\caption{Confusion matrices of the best base versus the best hybrid (in-domain,
row-normalised).}
\label{fig:confusion_best_models}
\end{figure}

The row-normalised confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_best_models}) and the per-class F1 scores (Figure~\ref{fig:per_class_f1}) localise where the hybrid helps and where the problem remains open.
Relative to the best baseline, the hybrid improves recall on the ordinal extreme \textbf{Highly\nobreakdash-Engage} ($0.38\to0.51$) and on \textbf{Disengage} ($0.45\to0.55$), while keeping \emph{Engage} near 0.90 ($0.91\to0.90$).
However, the rarest class \textbf{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage} stays unsolved and is slightly traded away (base recall $0.40\to$ hybrid 0.37): the model reallocates capacity toward the more frequent extreme.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{per_class_f1.png}
\caption{Per-class F1: best base versus best hybrid.}
\label{fig:per_class_f1}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
The hybrid's in-domain improvement is real but \textbf{uneven}: it strengthens the well-populated upper-engagement region and the middle disengaged class, but the $\sim$2--3\%-frequency \emph{Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage} class remains the principal open challenge.
This is the in-domain picture; Chapter~5 shows it becomes more severe still once the model leaves its training corpus.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:results-summary}

This chapter developed the model in-domain and fixed the experimental frame for the thesis.
The baseline comparison (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}) established that the strongest monolithic baseline is the OpenFace TCN at QWK 0.537, that loss choice is a Pareto trade-off in which cross-entropy gives the best ordinal agreement, and that QWK and macro-accuracy --- not accuracy --- must be the primary metrics; from this it selected cross-entropy as the training loss, those two metrics as the yardstick, and CMOSE as the development corpus.
Under that frame, the semantic-group hybrid as a family exceeds the baseline and is best when fused with I3D (QWK 0.605), with head pose the only group preferring a specific encoder, a TCN (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, RQ1--RQ3).
The error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:error}) showed the gain is concentrated in the upper-engagement region, with Highly\nobreakdash-Disengage the remaining open problem.
Chapter~5 takes this best in-domain model out of domain and asks whether any of it transfers to unseen corpora and to real, self-collected data (RQ4).
