\section{Overview}
\label{sec:baseline-overview}

This chapter studies the \emph{monolithic baseline} models --- the standard practice of pushing a feature sequence through a single temporal encoder (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) --- and in doing so fixes the methodological frame that the rest of the thesis depends on.
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} evaluates the five baselines under the three losses on \emph{all six metrics} of Section~\ref{sec:eval}.
The metrics turn out to disagree about which model is best; the section fixes \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the primary metrics and the baseline bar a new architecture must clear, and defers the decisive evidence for that choice --- the case against accuracy --- to the cross-dataset study at the end of the chapter.
Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff} examines, on the primary metrics alone, what each training loss trades, and selects the development loss.
Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor} explains why the architecture study to follow is anchored on CMOSE.
Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} then takes the baselines out of domain with the 3$\times$3 cross-dataset matrix: it shows that engagement models do not transfer off-the-shelf, delivers the deferred disproof of accuracy --- accuracy stays deceptively high in exactly the cells where every chance-corrected metric collapses --- and isolates the single most effective design lever for an unknown target.
Accuracy is therefore discussed only in Sections~\ref{sec:baseline-results} and~\ref{sec:crossdomain}; everywhere else the chapter argues exclusively from the primary metrics.
Throughout, the class imbalance characterised in Section~\ref{sec:data} is the backdrop: it is the reason the metrics disagree, and the reason the rare disengaged classes recur as the failure mode.

\section{In-Domain Results on All Metrics}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

\subsection{Models and losses}
\label{sec:baseline-allmetrics}

Five monolithic baselines --- four built on the OpenFace descriptor (an MLP, a TCN, an LSTM, and a Transformer) and one on the I3D clip vector (the I3D MLP) --- are trained and tested in-domain on CMOSE under each of the three losses.
Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_all_metrics} and Table~\ref{tab:T2_base_indomain} report the complete grid on all six metrics of Section~\ref{sec:eval}: accuracy, macro-accuracy, MAE, macro-MAE, Cohen's kappa, and QWK.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{base_models_all_metrics.png}
\caption{The five base models under the three losses on all six metrics, in-domain on
CMOSE. The metrics disagree on the winner: accuracy, MAE, and Cohen's kappa favour the
I3D MLP under cross-entropy, QWK favours the OpenFace TCN, and the macro metrics peak
under the balanced losses.}
\label{fig:base_models_all_metrics}
\end{figure}
\input{Table/T2_base_indomain}

\textbf{The six metrics do not agree on a winner.}
Read column by column, the grid splits into three camps.
The \emph{micro-averaged} metrics, which weight every clip equally and are therefore dominated by the majority \emph{Engage} class, all pick the \textbf{I3D MLP under cross-entropy}: accuracy 0.773, MAE 0.244, and Cohen's kappa 0.436.
The \emph{order-aware} QWK instead ranks the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy} first (0.537 against the I3D MLP's 0.519): because QWK weights an error by the squared distance between predicted and true level, it rewards models that respect the ordinal scale rather than models that concentrate on the majority class.
The \emph{macro-averaged} metrics, finally, peak in a different corner of the grid altogether --- the \textbf{I3D MLP under the ordinal loss} (macro-accuracy 0.635, macro-MAE 0.458) --- because only the rebalanced losses recover the rare disengaged classes that macro averaging weights equally.

\textbf{Why they disagree.}
The disagreement is a direct symptom of the class imbalance of Section~\ref{sec:data}: 69.4\% of CMOSE is \emph{Engage}, so a model that leans on the majority class scores well on every micro metric while contributing little on the classes a monitoring system exists to catch.
A single in-domain table cannot say which camp of metrics to believe --- each is internally consistent --- so the question becomes empirical: \emph{which metrics predict behaviour that holds up beyond the training corpus?}
That question is answered by the cross-dataset study of Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}, which shows that the models the micro metrics favour collapse to chance-level agreement out of domain while their accuracy stays deceptively high --- accuracy does not measure what survives.

\textbf{The frame this section fixes.}
Anticipating that result, the thesis adopts \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the three primary metrics --- QWK because it is chance-corrected, respects the class order, and cannot be inflated by majority prediction; macro-accuracy because it exposes minority-class recall directly; and macro-MAE (lower is better) because it measures \emph{how far} a prediction misses on the ordinal scale, averaged per class so that errors on the rare disengaged classes are not drowned out.
The three are complementary --- an agreement coefficient, a balanced recall, and a balanced ordinal distance --- so a model is judged strong only when all three agree.
Accuracy and micro-MAE remain in the tables for comparability with the literature but are not used to select models, and outside this section they reappear only in Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}, where their failure is demonstrated.
On the headline primary metric the strongest baseline is the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy at QWK 0.537}: this is the bar the proposed architecture of Chapter~5 must clear.

\subsection{OpenFace versus I3D}
\label{sec:openface-vs-i3d}

The baselines are built on two feature families of very different natures, and the contrast between them --- visible across the panels of Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_all_metrics} --- shapes the hybrid design that follows.
The \textbf{OpenFace} descriptor is a per-frame, structured account of facial behaviour --- gaze, eye and face landmarks, head pose, and action units --- so an OpenFace baseline sees the \emph{temporal dynamics} of the face and is encoded by a sequence model (here the TCN, LSTM, or Transformer).
The \textbf{I3D} feature is a single clip-level vector summarising global appearance and motion; the I3D baseline is therefore an MLP over one pooled vector, with no per-frame temporal structure to exploit.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{openface_vs_i3d.png}
\caption{Best OpenFace encoder (the TCN) versus the I3D MLP on the three primary metrics,
in-domain on CMOSE under cross-entropy: OpenFace wins all three.}
\label{fig:openface_vs_i3d}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:openface_vs_i3d} contrasts the strongest OpenFace baseline (the TCN) with the I3D MLP on the three primary metrics under cross-entropy.
The OpenFace TCN wins on \emph{all three}: QWK (0.537 versus 0.519), macro-accuracy (0.535 versus 0.526), and macro-MAE (0.547 versus 0.585, lower is better).
The fine-grained, per-frame facial dynamics it encodes carry the cues --- a head turning away, gaze drifting off-screen --- that separate the rarer disengaged levels, which the single pooled I3D vector blurs.
Yet the two families are \textbf{complementary rather than redundant}.
The I3D MLP's strength lies elsewhere in the grid: through its alignment with the majority \emph{Engage} class it tops every micro metric (accuracy 0.773, MAE 0.244), and it is the most \emph{reweightable} baseline, recovering the best balanced figures of any model under the ordinal loss (macro-accuracy 0.635, macro-MAE 0.458), whereas the OpenFace encoders gain less from reweighting.
Global appearance/motion and fine ordinal facial structure are thus captured best by different families --- the direct motivation for \emph{fusing} the two streams in the hybrid of Chapter~5 rather than choosing between them.

\textbf{Conclusion.}
The six metrics split into camps and crown different winners, which is itself the first warning against reading a single headline number.
The thesis judges every model by \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} --- with the decisive case against accuracy deferred to Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} --- and the bar for Chapter~5 is the OpenFace TCN's \textbf{QWK 0.537}.
The two feature families are complementary, motivating their fusion.

\section{Loss Trade-off}
\label{sec:loss-tradeoff}

The comparison above was read mostly under cross-entropy, but the loss is itself a design choice.
This section examines, \emph{on the three primary metrics only}, what each loss trades, and from it fixes the remaining frame decision: the loss on which the architecture is developed.

\textbf{Each loss trades the primary metrics against each other.}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff} plots, for every baseline, how each primary metric moves as the loss is rebalanced from cross-entropy through weighted cross-entropy to the ordinal loss.
The pattern is consistent and is the central observation of the section: as the loss is rebalanced, \textbf{macro-accuracy rises and macro-MAE drops (improves)}, while \textbf{QWK falls} for four of the five baselines --- the balanced losses buy minority-class recall and a smaller balanced ordinal error at the cost of the chance-corrected agreement QWK measures.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{loss_metric_tradeoff.png}
\caption{What each loss trades: the three primary metrics for every baseline across the
three losses, in-domain on CMOSE. Macro-accuracy and macro-MAE improve as the loss is
rebalanced, while QWK is generally highest under cross-entropy.}
\label{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

The same trade-off can be read as a Pareto frontier within the primary metrics themselves.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_pareto_macroacc} plots QWK against macro-accuracy and Figure~\ref{fig:loss_pareto_mae} plots QWK against macro-MAE; in both, the cross-entropy points sit toward high QWK, and the weighted-CE and ordinal points move toward better balance.
For the I3D MLP, for instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss lifts macro-accuracy from 0.526 to 0.635 and improves macro-MAE from 0.585 to 0.458 --- the best balanced figures of any baseline --- at the cost of QWK falling from 0.519 to 0.487.
There is no loss that is best on all three primaries at once: the choice is a genuine Pareto trade-off, and for engagement monitoring --- where missing disengagement is costly --- the balanced losses are a defensible operating point despite their weaker agreement.

\textbf{Decision --- the development loss.}
The strongest ordinal agreement of the whole baseline grid is obtained under \textbf{cross-entropy} (the bar-setting OpenFace TCN at QWK 0.537); the balanced losses trade the headline primary metric for minority recall rather than improving it.
Cross-entropy is therefore the natural loss on which to develop the architecture: it maximises QWK and isolates the effect of the \emph{architecture} from the confound of the loss.
The semantic-group hybrid of Chapter~5 is consequently trained with cross-entropy, and the balanced losses are revisited where they matter most --- recovering rare-class recall and surviving distribution shift.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce} shows representative training and validation loss curves; all baselines converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch cap, so the differences above are architectural rather than optimisation artefacts.

\begin{figure}[ht]

\caption{Representative training/validation loss curves (CMOSE, cross-entropy).}
\label{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Within the primary metrics the loss choice is a Pareto trade-off: cross-entropy gives the best QWK while the balanced losses buy macro-accuracy and macro-MAE.
The thesis develops the architecture under \textbf{cross-entropy}, the loss that maximises the headline primary metric and isolates the effect of the architecture.

\section{Development Corpus}
\label{sec:cmose-anchor}

The comparisons above, and the architecture study of Chapter~5, are conducted on CMOSE rather than DAiSEE for a principled reason: DAiSEE carries far weaker in-domain ordinal signal.
Table~\ref{tab:T7_indomain_datasets} and Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee} show that the best in-domain model reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535 versus 0.289, and macro-MAE 0.547 versus 1.226 --- more than twice the ordinal error).
On DAiSEE the best models barely exceed chance-level ordinal agreement, so architecture differences there would be dominated by label noise rather than model quality.
CMOSE is the only corpus on which architecture choices can be compared meaningfully; DAiSEE re-enters the study as a \emph{transfer} source and target in the cross-dataset matrix below and in Chapter~5, not as a development corpus.

\section{Cross-Dataset Generalization}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

In-domain numbers are not the deployment question.
This section takes the baselines out of domain --- training on one corpus and testing on another --- to ask whether any of the in-domain ordinal signal survives a change of corpus.
It also settles the question Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} left open: whether the models the micro metrics favoured were genuinely strong, or merely aligned with a class prior.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{crossdomain_base.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best base model per cell, on the three primary
metrics: QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE (lower is better).}
\label{fig:crossdomain_base}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The collapse.}
The 3$\times$3 heatmaps (Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} and Table~\ref{tab:T3_crossdomain}) are the central baseline generalisation result.
\textbf{On the diagonal} (train and test on the same corpus) QWK is healthy (CMOSE 0.54), macro-accuracy is well above chance (CMOSE 0.64), and ordinal error is low (CMOSE macro-MAE 0.46).
\textbf{Off the diagonal all three collapse together}: CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE QWK $=0.02$ and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE $=0.05$ (essentially chance-level agreement); macro-accuracy falls toward the 0.25 chance floor (DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.29); and macro-MAE inflates toward a full class (CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE 0.77, DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.99) even for the best models, against $\approx0.46$ on the diagonal.
The collapse is roughly symmetric --- neither public corpus transfers to the other --- which rules out the simpler reading that one corpus is a superset of the other and points instead to a genuine shift in both label priors and recording conditions.
\input{Table/T3_crossdomain}

\textbf{The disproof of accuracy.}
Here is the evidence Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} promised.
\textbf{Raw accuracy hides the collapse entirely}: in Table~\ref{tab:T3_crossdomain} the CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE accuracy is a deceptively reasonable 0.49 and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.69 --- in the very cells where QWK reads 0.02 and 0.05.
The mechanism is the one the in-domain grid hinted at: predicting near the majority class scores well on any imbalanced target, so a model can lose every trace of transferable ordinal structure and still post a respectable accuracy by inheriting a class prior that happens to overlap the target's majority.
The gap between the two numbers is itself the diagnostic: a single accuracy figure would have reported a partial success, while the chance-corrected, order-aware QWK reports the failure that is actually there.
This is the quantitative justification for the metric frame of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}: models selected for accuracy are precisely the ones whose apparent quality evaporates under distribution shift, so accuracy is excluded from every analysis in this thesis outside this section and the all-metrics overview.

\textbf{The lever: pool the source corpora.}
The most actionable cell is the unseen \textbf{Private} column: training on the \textbf{Combined} corpus gives the best private-set QWK (0.285), beating DAiSEE-only (0.256) and CMOSE-only (0.190).
Pooling source datasets is a simple, effective mitigation for an unknown target distribution: the union of CMOSE and DAiSEE spans a wider range of label priors than either alone, so an unseen target sits closer to the pooled distribution.
This is the single most effective design lever for an unknown target, and it is carried into Chapter~5, where it is combined with the architecture contribution on the decisive private set (\textbf{RQ4}).

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Engagement classifiers built on the standard monolithic recipe do not generalise across datasets: off-diagonal QWK falls to near zero even where accuracy looks acceptable, which both establishes that these models are \textbf{not portable off-the-shelf} and completes the case for the primary metrics fixed in Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}.
Among the available levers, \emph{pooling source corpora} is the most effective simple remedy for an unknown target.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:baseline-summary}

This chapter fixed the experimental frame and characterised the monolithic baselines.
The all-metrics comparison (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}) showed that the six metrics disagree on the best model --- the micro metrics favour majority-aligned models, QWK favours the OpenFace TCN, and the macro metrics favour the rebalanced losses --- and from that disagreement fixed \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the primary metrics, with the strongest baseline the OpenFace TCN at \textbf{QWK 0.537}, the bar the hybrid must beat; it also showed that the OpenFace and I3D feature families are complementary, motivating their fusion.
The loss study (Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}) showed that, within the primary metrics, the loss choice is a Pareto trade-off in which cross-entropy gives the best QWK, and selected \textbf{cross-entropy} as the development loss.
CMOSE was identified as the only corpus with enough in-domain signal to separate architectures (Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor}).
The cross-dataset study (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}) showed that baselines do not transfer off-the-shelf --- off-diagonal QWK collapses to near zero --- and delivered the deferred disproof of accuracy: accuracy stays deceptively high in exactly the cells where the ordinal signal disappears, vindicating the metric frame; corpus pooling emerged as the most effective simple mitigation.
Chapter~5 now introduces the semantic-group hybrid and, under exactly this frame, asks whether decomposing the OpenFace descriptor beats these baselines in-domain and whether the gain compounds with pooling on unseen, real-world data.
