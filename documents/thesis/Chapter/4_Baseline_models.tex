\section{Overview}
\label{sec:baseline-overview}

This chapter studies the \emph{monolithic baseline} models --- a feature sequence pushed through a single temporal encoder (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) --- and fixes the methodological frame the rest of the thesis depends on.
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} fixes the \textbf{evaluation protocol}: the metrics, the development corpus, and the development loss; the two arguments that require out-of-domain evidence --- that accuracy cannot detect cross-corpus collapse, and that the loss ranking is stable under shift --- are stated there as claims and proved in Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop}.
Under the fixed protocol, Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} ranks the five baselines and sets the bar the proposed architecture must clear; Section~\ref{sec:agreement} descends from scores to individual predictions and asks whether models with similar scores give the \emph{same answers} --- the analysis that motivates the hybrid design of Chapter~5; and Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} takes the baselines out of domain with the 3$\times$3 cross-dataset matrix.

\textbf{How results are aggregated.}
Two conventions hold in both results chapters: a \emph{winner} is the \textbf{maximum} over the tunable axis (the loss, and in Chapter~5 the per-group encoder assignment), a hyperparameter selected on validation data, while a claim about a \emph{family} of configurations is reported as \textbf{means and distributions}.
Every configuration is trained once with a fixed seed (Section~\ref{sec:training}), so a reported mean is a mean over the design grid --- robustness to design choices, not a multi-seed noise estimate.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:protocol-metrics}

Engagement is an \emph{ordinal}, severely \emph{imbalanced} four-class problem: the levels lie on a line, and 69.4\% of CMOSE is the single class \emph{Engage} (Table~\ref{tab:T3_1_dataset_stats}).
Raw accuracy ignores both properties --- it rewards majority-class collapse and counts a one-level miss the same as a three-level miss --- whereas QWK is chance-corrected and weights every error by the squared distance between predicted and true levels (Section~\ref{sec:metrics-lit}).
On first principles, accuracy is the wrong yardstick for this task; the in-domain data say the same thing twice over.

First, \textbf{the leaderboard winner depends on the metric}.
Table~\ref{tab:T4_1_per_metric_winner} reports which of the fifteen baseline configurations (five architectures $\times$ three losses) each of the six metrics declares best in-domain on CMOSE, and no two camps agree: QWK crowns the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy} (0.537), both macro metrics crown the \textbf{I3D MLP under the ordinal loss} (macro-accuracy 0.635, macro-MAE 0.458), and accuracy, micro-MAE, and Cohen's kappa all crown the \textbf{I3D MLP under cross-entropy} (0.773, 0.244, 0.436).
A metric choice is never innocent, so it must be argued, not defaulted.
\input{Table/T4_1_per_metric_winner}

Second, \textbf{the six metrics form two blocks, bridged only by QWK}.
Figure~\ref{fig:metric_correlation_base} reports the Kendall rank correlation between every pair of metrics over the fifteen configurations: a \emph{micro} block --- accuracy and micro-MAE ($\tau=0.98$), dominated by the majority class --- and a \emph{macro} block --- macro-accuracy and macro-MAE ($\tau=0.89$) --- that are nearly orthogonal to each other (cross-block $\tau\approx0.01$--$0.10$).
\textbf{QWK is the only metric with moderate-to-strong agreement with both blocks} ($\tau=0.49$--$0.60$): the compromise metric, sensitive to ordinal distance without being enslaved to the majority class.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{metric_correlation_base.png}
\caption{Rank agreement (Kendall $\tau$) between the six metrics over the fifteen
baseline configurations, in-domain on CMOSE (MAE metrics sign-flipped so positive $\tau$
means agreement). The metrics form a micro block and a macro block that barely agree;
QWK is the only metric correlated with both.}
\label{fig:metric_correlation_base}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Protocol.}
The thesis adopts \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the three primary metrics, with QWK as the headline; a model is judged strong only when all three agree.
Accuracy and micro-MAE remain in the tables for comparability with the literature but never select models.
\emph{Deferred evidence:} the decisive argument against accuracy --- that it cannot even detect the collapse of a model's ordinal signal under distribution shift --- is delivered in Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop}.

\subsection{Development Corpus}
\label{sec:cmose-anchor}

Table~\ref{tab:T4_2_indomain_datasets} compares the two in-domain cells of the evaluation matrix: the best in-domain model reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535 versus 0.289, macro-MAE 0.547 versus 1.226 --- more than twice the ordinal error), while DAiSEE's raw accuracy of 0.548 hides the failure --- a first taste of the foil accuracy was retained to play.
A corpus on which no model rises meaningfully above chance cannot separate good architectures from bad ones: whatever ranking it produces is dominated by label noise.
\textbf{CMOSE is therefore the development corpus}; DAiSEE re-enters as a transfer source and target in Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} and in Chapter~5, where its difficulty is informative rather than obstructive.

\subsection{Development Loss}
\label{sec:loss-tradeoff}

In-domain, the loss axis is a clean trade within the primary metrics.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff} shows, for every baseline, that rebalancing the loss from cross-entropy through weighted cross-entropy to the ordinal loss \textbf{raises macro-accuracy and improves macro-MAE} while \textbf{QWK falls} for four of the five baselines: the balanced losses buy minority-class recall at the cost of chance-corrected agreement.
The trade is genuinely Pareto --- no loss is best on all three primaries at once.
For the I3D MLP, for instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss lifts macro-accuracy from 0.526 to 0.635 and improves macro-MAE from 0.585 to 0.458 --- the best balanced figures of any baseline --- while QWK falls from 0.519 to 0.487.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{loss_metric_tradeoff.png}
\caption{What each loss trades: the three primary metrics for every baseline across the
three losses, in-domain on CMOSE. Macro-accuracy and macro-MAE improve as the loss is
rebalanced, while QWK is generally highest under cross-entropy.}
\label{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Protocol.}
The strongest ordinal agreement of the whole grid is obtained under \textbf{cross-entropy} (best-architecture QWK by loss: CE 0.537, weighted CE 0.500, ordinal 0.487), so \textbf{cross-entropy is the development loss}: it maximises the headline metric and isolates the effect of the architecture from the confound of the loss; the hybrid of Chapter~5 is consequently trained with it.
\emph{Deferred evidence:} Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop} shows this CE $>$ weighted CE $>$ ordinal ranking is preserved intact under domain shift --- which retroactively makes cross-entropy the \emph{reliable} choice, not merely the convenient one.
The training and validation loss curves of every configuration converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch cap (Section~\ref{sec:training}), so the differences reported in this chapter are architectural rather than optimisation artefacts.

\section{In-Domain Results}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

Five monolithic baselines --- four on the OpenFace descriptor (an MLP, a TCN, an LSTM, and a Transformer) and one on the I3D clip vector (the I3D MLP) --- are trained and tested in-domain on CMOSE under each of the three losses; Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_all_metrics} reports the complete grid on all six metrics.
On the primary metrics, the strongest baseline is the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy at QWK 0.537} (max-over-loss, per Section~\ref{sec:baseline-overview}): the bar the proposed architecture of Chapter~5 must clear.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{base_models_all_metrics.png}
\caption{The five base models under the three losses on all six metrics, in-domain on
CMOSE. The metrics disagree on the winner: accuracy, MAE, and Cohen's kappa favour the
I3D MLP under cross-entropy, QWK favours the OpenFace TCN, and the macro metrics peak
under the balanced losses.}
\label{fig:base_models_all_metrics}
\end{figure}

The grid adds two structural observations.
First, \textbf{the loss axis decides which metric family a model wins}: cross-entropy wins the micro and agreement metrics, while only the rebalanced losses recover the rare disengaged classes that macro averaging weights equally --- a direct symptom of the class imbalance.
Second, \textbf{the architecture axis decides the feature story}: the OpenFace TCN's per-frame facial dynamics carry the cues --- a head turning away, gaze drifting off-screen --- that separate the rarer disengaged levels and win the ordinal primary, while the I3D MLP, over a single pooled appearance/motion vector, aligns with the majority \emph{Engage} class and tops every micro metric.
On the headline metric the two are nearly tied --- \textbf{QWK 0.537 versus 0.519} --- and two near-tied models built on \emph{disjoint feature families} pose a question the score table cannot answer: are they interchangeable, or do they succeed on different clips?

\section{Prediction Agreement}
\label{sec:agreement}

Scores summarise \emph{how often} a model is right; they say nothing about \emph{which clips} it is right on.
This section descends to individual predictions on the 1{,}221 in-domain CMOSE test clips, first across the whole grid, then for the two feature families specifically.

\subsection{Agreement between Configurations}
\label{sec:agreement-configs}

Figure~\ref{fig:agreement_base_models} reports the pairwise Cohen's kappa between the predictions of every pair of configurations.
Mean pairwise agreement is only $\kappa=0.27$--$0.39$ --- far below what the clustered scores of Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_all_metrics} suggest --- and sharing the architecture ($\kappa=0.391$) or the loss ($\kappa=0.374$) raises it only mildly over sharing neither ($\kappa=0.273$): the fifteen configurations are genuinely different hypotheses about the data, not reparametrisations of one another.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{agreement_base_models.png}
\caption{Pairwise prediction agreement (Cohen's $\kappa$) between all fifteen baseline
configurations on the in-domain CMOSE test set, in model-major blocks. The diagonal
blocks are the same architecture under different losses; agreement is moderate at best
everywhere.}
\label{fig:agreement_base_models}
\end{figure}

The practical consequence is \textbf{headroom}.
The best single configuration classifies 77.3\% of the test clips correctly, but \textbf{at least one of the fifteen is correct on 97.6\%} (the oracle); only 2.4\% of clips defeat every model.
Yet plain majority voting over the fifteen lands at 73.5\% --- \emph{below} the best single model --- because the errors are too decorrelated for unweighted voting to harvest.
The headroom exists, but claiming it requires a \emph{learned} combination of complementary views of the input --- precisely the design premise of the semantic-group hybrid of Chapter~5, which fuses separately-encoded behavioural streams instead of averaging finished predictions.

\subsection{OpenFace versus I3D}
\label{sec:openface-vs-i3d}

The two families behind the near-tie of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} are of very different natures: \textbf{OpenFace} is a per-frame, structured account of facial behaviour (gaze, eye and face landmarks, head pose, action units), while \textbf{I3D} is a single clip-level vector of global appearance and motion.
Table~\ref{tab:T4_3_openface_vs_i3d} compares their best cross-entropy models: the OpenFace TCN wins all three primary metrics, so by scores alone the I3D MLP looks merely redundant.
\input{Table/T4_3_openface_vs_i3d}

The agreement map says otherwise.
In Figure~\ref{fig:agreement_base_models}, the I3D MLP's row is the palest in the grid: its agreement with the OpenFace TCN is among the \emph{lowest} of any pair, despite the two posting the closest scores.
Two models that score alike but agree this rarely must succeed on different clips, and the clip-level decomposition confirms it: against the OpenFace TCN, the I3D MLP has the largest exclusive-correct share of any pair in the grid --- \textbf{8.6\% of all test clips are clips only I3D gets right} --- and the smallest both-wrong share (15.3\%).
The pair's oracle accuracy is 84.7\%, +7.4 points over the best single model and the largest pair gain in the grid.

\textbf{Conclusion.}
The behaviour descriptors and the appearance features are looking at genuinely \textbf{different evidence}: the families fail on different clips.
This is the empirical motivation for \emph{fusing} the two streams in the hybrid of Chapter~5 --- a motivation the score table alone could never supply.

\section{Cross-Dataset Generalization}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

In-domain numbers are not the deployment question.
This section trains on one corpus and tests on another, with three aims: measure whether any ordinal signal survives a change of corpus, close the two arguments the protocol deferred, and ask whether in-domain strength predicts generalisation at all.

\subsection{The 3$\times$3 Matrix}
\label{sec:crossdomain-matrix}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{crossdomain_base.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best base model per cell, on the three primary
metrics --- QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE (lower is better) --- plus raw accuracy,
the deceptive foil: accuracy stays high in exactly the off-diagonal cells where every
chance-corrected metric collapses.}
\label{fig:crossdomain_base}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The collapse.}
The 3$\times$3 heatmaps of Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} are the central baseline generalisation result.
\textbf{On the diagonal} (train and test on the same corpus) QWK is healthy (CMOSE 0.54), macro-accuracy is well above chance (CMOSE 0.64), and ordinal error is low (CMOSE macro-MAE 0.46).
\textbf{Off the diagonal all three collapse together}: CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE QWK $=0.02$ and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE $=0.05$ (chance-level agreement), macro-accuracy falls toward the 0.25 chance floor, and macro-MAE inflates toward a full class.
The collapse is roughly symmetric --- neither public corpus transfers to the other --- which rules out one corpus being a superset of the other and points to a genuine shift in both label priors and recording conditions.

\textbf{The architecture ranking is reshuffled.}
Comparing, per architecture, the in-domain QWK with the mean QWK over the \emph{unseen-target} cells --- the cells whose test corpus contributed no training data (the two cross-corpus cells and the private column) --- the in-domain order TCN 0.537 $>$ I3D 0.519 $>$ Transformer 0.443 $>$ MLP 0.399 $>$ LSTM 0.387 becomes TCN $\approx$ Transformer $>$ LSTM $>$ \textbf{I3D} $>$ MLP on unseen targets.
\textbf{The I3D MLP falls from second place to fourth}: its global appearance features bind to the corpus, while the OpenFace behaviour descriptors carry more corpus-invariant signal.

\textbf{The lever: pool the source corpora.}
The most actionable cell is the unseen \textbf{Private} column: training on the \textbf{Combined} corpus gives the best private-set QWK (0.285), beating DAiSEE-only (0.256) and CMOSE-only (0.190), while keeping near-in-domain CMOSE performance (0.477, the Combined$\rightarrow$CMOSE cell of Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base}).
The union of CMOSE and DAiSEE spans a wider range of label priors than either alone, so an unseen target sits closer to the pooled distribution.
This is the single most effective design lever for an unknown target, and Chapter~5 combines it with the architecture contribution on the decisive private set (\textbf{RQ4}).

\subsection{Closing the Loop: Metric and Loss Reliability}
\label{sec:closing-loop}

\textbf{Accuracy cannot see the collapse (completes Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}).}
In the accuracy panel of Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base}, CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE posts a deceptively reasonable 0.49 and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.69 --- in the very cells where the QWK panel reads 0.02 and 0.05, because predicting near the majority class scores well on any imbalanced target.
A metric that reports business-as-usual while the ordinal signal is gone is a blindfold, not a safety net: \textbf{accuracy is the least reliable metric in this study and QWK} --- the only block-bridging metric, and the one that detects the collapse --- \textbf{the most}.

\textbf{The loss ranking survives the shift (completes Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}).}
On the unseen-target cells the best-architecture QWK by loss follows the same CE $>$ weighted CE $>$ ordinal order as in-domain.
The loss is the only factor in the grid whose ranking transfers (the architecture ranking reshuffles): choosing cross-entropy in-domain remains the right choice out of domain, which retroactively validates fixing it as the development loss.

\subsection{In-Domain versus Transfer}
\label{sec:indomain-vs-transfer}

Does in-domain strength predict transfer?
Across the thirty trained baseline configurations, the Spearman rank correlation between in-domain QWK and mean QWK over the unseen-target cells is only $\boldsymbol{\rho=0.28}$ --- too weak to select models on, so model selection for an unknown target must lean on the pooling lever rather than the in-domain leaderboard.
Chapter~5 sharpens this warning on the hybrid population, where the correlation even turns negative (Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_vs_generalization_hybrid}).

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:baseline-summary}

The evaluation protocol fixed \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the primary metrics, \textbf{CMOSE} as the development corpus, and \textbf{cross-entropy} as the development loss; under it, the strongest baseline is the OpenFace TCN at \textbf{QWK 0.537} --- the bar the hybrid must beat.
The prediction-level analysis showed the configurations agree far less than their scores suggest (oracle 97.6\%) and that the OpenFace and I3D families fail on \emph{different} clips, the direct motivation for fusing them; the cross-dataset study showed baselines do not transfer off-the-shelf, vindicated the metric and loss choices, and made corpus pooling the most effective simple mitigation.
Chapter~5 now asks whether learning to fuse the complementary streams beats these baselines, and whether the gain compounds with pooling on unseen data.
