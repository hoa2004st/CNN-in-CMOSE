\section{Overview}
\label{sec:baseline-overview}

This chapter studies the \emph{monolithic baseline} models --- the standard practice of pushing a feature sequence through a single temporal encoder (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) --- and in doing so fixes the methodological frame the rest of the thesis depends on.
It is organised so that every convention is justified \emph{before} it is used.
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} fixes the \textbf{evaluation protocol}: which metrics to read, which corpus to develop on, and which loss to develop with.
Each choice is argued from first principles and from evidence that is itself in-domain; the two arguments that require out-of-domain evidence --- that accuracy cannot detect cross-corpus collapse, and that the loss ranking is stable under shift --- are stated there as claims and proved in Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop}, so the reader is told exactly where each promise is kept.
Under the fixed protocol, Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} ranks the five baselines and sets the bar the proposed architecture must clear.
Section~\ref{sec:agreement} then descends from scores to individual predictions and asks whether models with similar scores give the \emph{same answers} --- the analysis that motivates the hybrid design of Chapter~5.
Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} takes the baselines out of domain with the 3$\times$3 cross-dataset matrix, closes the two deferred arguments, and asks whether in-domain strength predicts generalisation at all.
Throughout, the class imbalance characterised in Section~\ref{sec:data} is the backdrop: it is the reason the metrics disagree, and the reason the rare disengaged classes recur as the failure mode.

\textbf{How results are aggregated.}
Two conventions, used in both results chapters, are fixed here.
When the chapter crowns a \emph{winner}, it takes the \textbf{maximum} over the tunable axis: the loss (and, in Chapter~5, the per-group encoder assignment) is a hyperparameter selected on validation data, so ``which architecture is best'' means ``best achievable when tuned''.
Averaging over losses would punish an architecture for losses it would never ship with, and would mix objectives that deliberately optimise different metrics.
When a claim concerns a \emph{family} of configurations (Chapter~5's 243-configuration ablation, the per-group marginals), the chapter reports \textbf{means and distributions}: nobody tunes 243 configurations, so a family-level claim must hold for the population, not for its lucky maximum.
Finally, every configuration is trained once with a fixed seed (Section~\ref{sec:training}), so wherever this thesis reports a mean it is a mean over the design grid --- a measure of robustness to design choices, not a multi-seed noise estimate.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Before any model is compared, three conventions must be fixed.
This section fixes them in order: the metrics (Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}), the development corpus (Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor}), and the development loss (Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}).

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:protocol-metrics}

\textbf{A-priori.}
Engagement is an \emph{ordinal}, severely \emph{imbalanced} four-class problem: the levels lie on a line, and 69.4\% of CMOSE is the single class \emph{Engage} (Section~\ref{sec:data}, Table~\ref{tab:T1_dataset_stats}).
Raw accuracy ignores both properties --- it rewards majority-class collapse and counts a one-level miss the same as a three-level miss --- whereas QWK is chance-corrected and weights every error by the squared distance between the predicted and true levels (Section~\ref{sec:metrics-lit}).
On first principles, then, accuracy is the wrong yardstick for this task and QWK the natural one.
The in-domain data say the same thing twice over.

\textbf{The leaderboard winner depends on the metric.}
Table~\ref{tab:T8_per_metric_winner} reports, for each of the six metrics, which of the fifteen baseline configurations (five architectures $\times$ three losses) it declares best in-domain on CMOSE.
No two camps agree: QWK crowns the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy} (0.537); both macro metrics crown the \textbf{I3D MLP under the ordinal loss} (macro-accuracy 0.635, macro-MAE 0.458); accuracy, micro-MAE, and Cohen's kappa all crown the \textbf{I3D MLP under cross-entropy} (0.773, 0.244, 0.436).
A metric choice is therefore never innocent --- each crowns its own champion --- so the choice must be argued, not defaulted.
\input{Table/T8_per_metric_winner}

\textbf{The six metrics form two blocks, bridged only by QWK.}
Figure~\ref{fig:metric_correlation_base} quantifies how far the metrics agree on the \emph{ranking} of the fifteen configurations, as the Kendall rank correlation between every pair of metrics.
The metrics split into a \emph{micro} block --- accuracy and micro-MAE, which rank the models almost identically ($\tau=0.98$) because both are dominated by the majority class --- and a \emph{macro} block --- macro-accuracy and macro-MAE ($\tau=0.89$) --- and the two blocks are nearly orthogonal to each other (cross-block $\tau\approx0.01$--$0.10$).
Choosing a metric from either block alone silently discards the other half of the picture.
\textbf{QWK is the only metric with moderate-to-strong agreement with both blocks} ($\tau=0.49$--$0.60$, and $\tau=0.79$ with Cohen's kappa): it is the compromise metric, sensitive to ordinal distance without being enslaved to the majority class.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{metric_correlation_base.png}
\caption{Rank agreement (Kendall $\tau$) between the six metrics over the fifteen
baseline configurations, in-domain on CMOSE (the MAE metrics are sign-flipped so that

\textbf{Protocol.}
The thesis adopts \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the three primary metrics --- an agreement coefficient, a balanced recall, and a balanced ordinal distance --- with QWK as the headline; a model is judged strong only when all three agree.
Accuracy and micro-MAE remain in the tables for comparability with the literature but are never used to select models.
\emph{Deferred evidence:} the decisive argument against accuracy --- that it cannot even detect the collapse of a model's ordinal signal under distribution shift --- requires the cross-dataset study and is delivered in Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop}.

\subsection{Development Corpus}
\label{sec:cmose-anchor}

This choice needs no deferred evidence: it rests entirely on the two in-domain cells of the evaluation matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:T7_indomain_datasets} and Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee} show that the best in-domain model reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535 versus 0.289, and macro-MAE 0.547 versus 1.226 --- more than twice the ordinal error), while DAiSEE's raw accuracy of 0.548 hides the failure --- a first taste of the foil that Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics} retained accuracy to play.
A corpus on which no model rises meaningfully above chance cannot separate good architectures from bad ones: whatever ranking it produces is dominated by label noise.

\textbf{Protocol.}
\textbf{CMOSE is the development corpus.}
DAiSEE re-enters the study as a transfer source and target in the cross-dataset matrix (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}) and in Chapter~5, where its difficulty is informative rather than obstructive.

\subsection{Development Loss}
\label{sec:loss-tradeoff}

In-domain, the loss axis is a clean trade within the primary metrics.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff} plots, for every baseline, how each primary metric moves as the loss is rebalanced from cross-entropy through weighted cross-entropy to the ordinal loss: \textbf{macro-accuracy rises and macro-MAE drops (improves)}, while \textbf{QWK falls} for four of the five baselines.
The balanced losses buy minority-class recall and a smaller balanced ordinal error at the cost of the chance-corrected agreement QWK measures.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{loss_metric_tradeoff.png}
\caption{What each loss trades: the three primary metrics for every baseline across the
three losses, in-domain on CMOSE. Macro-accuracy and macro-MAE improve as the loss is
rebalanced, while QWK is generally highest under cross-entropy.}
\label{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

The same trade-off can be read as a Pareto frontier within the primary metrics themselves.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_pareto_macroacc} plots QWK against macro-accuracy and Figure~\ref{fig:loss_pareto_mae} plots QWK against macro-MAE; in both, the cross-entropy points sit toward high QWK, and the weighted-CE and ordinal points move toward better balance.
For the I3D MLP, for instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss lifts macro-accuracy from 0.526 to 0.635 and improves macro-MAE from 0.585 to 0.458 --- the best balanced figures of any baseline --- at the cost of QWK falling from 0.519 to 0.487.
There is no loss that is best on all three primaries at once: the choice is a genuine Pareto trade-off, and for engagement monitoring --- where missing disengagement is costly --- the balanced losses remain a defensible operating point despite their weaker agreement.

\textbf{Protocol.}
The strongest ordinal agreement of the whole baseline grid is obtained under \textbf{cross-entropy} (best-architecture QWK by loss: CE 0.537, weighted CE 0.500, ordinal 0.487), so \textbf{cross-entropy is the development loss}: it maximises the headline primary metric and isolates the effect of the \emph{architecture} from the confound of the loss.
The semantic-group hybrid of Chapter~5 is consequently trained with cross-entropy.
\emph{Deferred evidence:} Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop} shows that this CE $>$ weighted CE $>$ ordinal ranking is preserved intact under domain shift --- the only loss effect that survives --- which retroactively makes cross-entropy the \emph{reliable} choice, not merely the convenient one.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce} shows representative training and validation loss curves; all baselines converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch cap, so the differences reported in this chapter are architectural rather than optimisation artefacts.

\section{In-Domain Results}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

With the protocol fixed, the architecture question has a clean answer.
Five monolithic baselines --- four built on the OpenFace descriptor (an MLP, a TCN, an LSTM, and a Transformer) and one on the I3D clip vector (the I3D MLP) --- are trained and tested in-domain on CMOSE under each of the three losses; Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_all_metrics} and Table~\ref{tab:T2_base_indomain} report the complete grid on all six metrics.
On the primary metrics, the strongest baseline is the \textbf{OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy at QWK 0.537} (max-over-loss, per the aggregation policy of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-overview}): this is the bar the proposed architecture of Chapter~5 must clear.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{base_models_all_metrics.png}
\caption{The five base models under the three losses on all six metrics, in-domain on
CMOSE. The metrics disagree on the winner: accuracy, MAE, and Cohen's kappa favour the
I3D MLP under cross-entropy, QWK favours the OpenFace TCN, and the macro metrics peak
under the balanced losses.}
\label{fig:base_models_all_metrics}
\end{figure}
\input{Table/T2_base_indomain}

The full grid adds two structural observations.
First, \textbf{the loss axis decides which metric family a model wins}: cross-entropy wins the micro and agreement metrics while the rebalanced losses win the macro metrics, because only the rebalanced losses recover the rare disengaged classes that macro averaging weights equally --- the per-metric winners of Table~\ref{tab:T8_per_metric_winner} are this pattern seen from the other side, and a direct symptom of the class imbalance of Section~\ref{sec:data}.
Second, \textbf{the architecture axis decides the feature story}.
The OpenFace TCN's fine-grained, per-frame facial dynamics carry the cues --- a head turning away, gaze drifting off-screen --- that separate the rarer disengaged levels and win the ordinal primary; the I3D MLP, an MLP over a single pooled appearance/motion vector, aligns with the majority \emph{Engage} class, tops every micro metric, and is the most reweightable baseline, recovering the best balanced figures of any model under the ordinal loss.
On the headline metric the two are nearly tied --- \textbf{QWK 0.537 versus 0.519} --- and two near-tied models built on \emph{disjoint feature families} pose a question the score table cannot answer: are they interchangeable, or do they succeed on different clips?
That question is the subject of the next section.

\section{Prediction Agreement}
\label{sec:agreement}

Scores summarise \emph{how often} a model is right; they say nothing about \emph{which clips} it is right on.
This section descends to the level of individual predictions on the 1{,}221 in-domain CMOSE test clips and asks whether the fifteen baseline configurations give the same answers --- first across the whole grid, then for the two feature families specifically.
The answers motivate the central design decision of Chapter~5.

\subsection{Agreement between Configurations}
\label{sec:agreement-configs}

Figure~\ref{fig:agreement_base_models} reports the pairwise Cohen's kappa between the predictions of every pair of configurations, and Table~\ref{tab:T9_agreement_stats} summarises the statistics.
Mean pairwise agreement is only $\kappa=0.27$--$0.39$ --- far below what the clustered scores of Table~\ref{tab:T2_base_indomain} suggest.
Sharing the architecture ($\kappa=0.391$) or the loss ($\kappa=0.374$) raises agreement only mildly over sharing neither ($\kappa=0.273$): the disagreement is driven by \emph{both} axes, so the fifteen configurations are genuinely different hypotheses about the data, not reparametrisations of one another.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{agreement_base_models.png}
\caption{Pairwise prediction agreement (Cohen's $\kappa$) between all fifteen baseline
configurations on the in-domain CMOSE test set, in model-major blocks. The diagonal
blocks are the same architecture under different losses; agreement is moderate at best
everywhere.}
\label{fig:agreement_base_models}
\end{figure}
\input{Table/T9_agreement_stats}

The practical consequence is \textbf{headroom}.
The best single configuration classifies 77.3\% of the test clips correctly, but \textbf{at least one of the fifteen is correct on 97.6\%} (the oracle); only 2.4\% of clips defeat every model.
Yet plain majority voting over the fifteen lands at 73.5\% --- \emph{below} the best single model --- because the errors are too decorrelated for unweighted voting to harvest.
The headroom exists, but claiming it requires a \emph{learned} combination of complementary views of the input.
That is precisely the design premise of the semantic-group hybrid of Chapter~5, which learns to fuse separately-encoded behavioural streams instead of averaging finished predictions.

\subsection{OpenFace versus I3D}
\label{sec:openface-vs-i3d}

The two feature families behind the near-tie of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} are of very different natures: the \textbf{OpenFace} descriptor is a per-frame, structured account of facial behaviour (gaze, eye and face landmarks, head pose, action units), while the \textbf{I3D} feature is a single clip-level vector summarising global appearance and motion.
Figure~\ref{fig:openface_vs_i3d} compares their best models on the three primary metrics --- the OpenFace TCN wins all three --- but the prediction-level analysis is what reveals their relationship.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{openface_vs_i3d.png}
\caption{Best OpenFace encoder (the TCN) versus the I3D MLP on the three primary metrics,
in-domain on CMOSE under cross-entropy: OpenFace wins all three.}
\label{fig:openface_vs_i3d}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_error_overlap} decomposes, clip by clip, where the OpenFace TCN and each other cross-entropy model are right and wrong.
Against the OpenFace TCN, the \textbf{I3D MLP has the largest exclusive-correct share} --- 8.6\% of all test clips are clips that \emph{only} I3D gets right, against 5--6\% for the OpenFace partners --- \textbf{and the smallest both-wrong share} (15.3\%, against 18--19\% within the OpenFace family).
The pair's oracle accuracy is 84.7\%, +7.4 points over the best single model and the largest pair gain in the grid (Table~\ref{tab:T9_agreement_stats}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{feature_error_overlap.png}
\caption{Error overlap between the OpenFace TCN and every other architecture (all under
cross-entropy, in-domain CMOSE): the share of test clips both classify correctly, only

share and the smallest both-wrong share --- the two feature families fail on different
clips.}
\label{fig:feature_error_overlap}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
The behaviour descriptors and the appearance features are looking at genuinely \textbf{different evidence}: the families fail on different clips.
This is the empirical motivation for \emph{fusing} the two streams in the hybrid of Chapter~5 --- a motivation the score table alone could never supply, since there the I3D MLP's 0.519 looks merely redundant next to the TCN's 0.537.

\section{Cross-Dataset Generalization}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

In-domain numbers are not the deployment question.
This section takes the baselines out of domain --- training on one corpus and testing on another --- with three aims: to measure whether any in-domain ordinal signal survives a change of corpus (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain-matrix}); to close the two arguments the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} deferred (Section~\ref{sec:closing-loop}); and to ask whether in-domain strength predicts generalisation at all (Section~\ref{sec:indomain-vs-transfer}).

\subsection{The 3$\times$3 Matrix}
\label{sec:crossdomain-matrix}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{crossdomain_base.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best base model per cell, on the three primary
metrics --- QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE (lower is better) --- plus raw accuracy,
the deceptive foil: accuracy stays high in exactly the off-diagonal cells where every
chance-corrected metric collapses.}
\label{fig:crossdomain_base}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The collapse.}
The 3$\times$3 heatmaps of Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} are the central baseline generalisation result.
\textbf{On the diagonal} (train and test on the same corpus) QWK is healthy (CMOSE 0.54), macro-accuracy is well above chance (CMOSE 0.64), and ordinal error is low (CMOSE macro-MAE 0.46).
\textbf{Off the diagonal all three collapse together}: CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE QWK $=0.02$ and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE $=0.05$ (essentially chance-level agreement); macro-accuracy falls toward the 0.25 chance floor (DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.29); and macro-MAE inflates toward a full class (CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE 0.77, DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.99) even for the best models, against $\approx0.46$ on the diagonal.
The collapse is roughly symmetric --- neither public corpus transfers to the other --- which rules out the simpler reading that one corpus is a superset of the other and points instead to a genuine shift in both label priors and recording conditions.

\textbf{The architecture ranking is reshuffled.}
The collapse spares no model, but it does not treat them equally.
Figure~\ref{fig:generalization_by_arch} compares, per architecture, the in-domain QWK with the mean QWK over the \emph{unseen-target} cells --- the cells whose test corpus contributed no training data (the two cross-corpus cells and the private column).
In-domain the order is TCN 0.537 $>$ I3D 0.519 $>$ Transformer 0.443 $>$ MLP 0.399 $>$ LSTM 0.387; on unseen targets it becomes TCN 0.112 $\approx$ Transformer 0.110 $>$ LSTM 0.096 $>$ \textbf{I3D 0.085} $>$ MLP 0.065.
\textbf{The I3D MLP falls from second place to fourth}: its global appearance features bind to the corpus, while the OpenFace behaviour descriptors carry more corpus-invariant signal.
In-domain performance and generalisation are therefore \emph{complementary} selection criteria along the architecture axis, not one criterion measured twice.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{generalization_by_arch.png}
\caption{Architecture ranking in-domain versus on the unseen-target cells (best loss per
cell). The in-domain order does not survive the shift: the I3D MLP drops from second to
fourth, while the OpenFace encoders hold their relative positions.}
\label{fig:generalization_by_arch}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The lever: pool the source corpora.}
The most actionable cell is the unseen \textbf{Private} column: training on the \textbf{Combined} corpus gives the best private-set QWK (0.285), beating DAiSEE-only (0.256) and CMOSE-only (0.190), while keeping near-in-domain CMOSE performance (0.477).
Pooling source datasets is a simple, effective mitigation for an unknown target distribution: the union of CMOSE and DAiSEE spans a wider range of label priors than either alone, so an unseen target sits closer to the pooled distribution.
This is the single most effective design lever for an unknown target, and it is carried into Chapter~5, where it is combined with the architecture contribution on the decisive private set (\textbf{RQ4}).

\subsection{Closing the Loop: Metric and Loss Reliability}
\label{sec:closing-loop}

The two claims deferred from the evaluation protocol can now be proved.

\textbf{Accuracy cannot see the collapse (completes Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}).}
In the accuracy panel of Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} the CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE accuracy is a deceptively reasonable 0.49 and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.69 --- in the very cells where the QWK panel reads 0.02 and 0.05.
The mechanism is the one the in-domain grid hinted at: predicting near the majority class scores well on any imbalanced target, so a model can lose every trace of transferable ordinal structure and still post a respectable accuracy by inheriting a class prior that happens to overlap the target's majority.
A metric that reports business-as-usual while the model has lost all ordinal signal is not a safety net but a blindfold.
Combined with its in-domain blindness to imbalance (DAiSEE's 0.548, Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor}), \textbf{accuracy is the least reliable metric in this study; QWK} --- the only block-bridging metric (Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}) and the one that detects the collapse --- \textbf{is the most}.
The protocol's metric choice stands confirmed, and models selected for accuracy are precisely the ones whose apparent quality evaporates under shift.

\textbf{The loss ranking survives the shift (completes Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}).}
Figure~\ref{fig:generalization_by_loss} repeats the per-loss comparison on the unseen-target cells: best-architecture QWK by loss is CE 0.160, weighted CE 0.099, ordinal 0.093 --- the same order as in-domain (0.537, 0.500, 0.487).
The loss is the only factor in the grid whose ranking transfers (the architecture ranking, by contrast, reshuffles --- Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain-matrix}).
Cross-entropy is therefore reliable in the strict sense that choosing it in-domain remains the right choice out of domain, which retroactively validates fixing it as the development loss.

\caption{Loss ranking in-domain versus on the unseen-target cells (best architecture per
cell): cross-entropy is best in both regimes --- the only factor ranking that survives
the shift.}
\label{fig:generalization_by_loss}
\end{figure}

\subsection{In-Domain versus Transfer}
\label{sec:indomain-vs-transfer}

A final question completes the generalisation picture: \emph{does in-domain strength predict transfer?}
Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_vs_generalization_base} plots, for every trained model (each configuration trained on CMOSE or on DAiSEE), its in-domain QWK against its mean QWK over its unseen-target cells.
The Spearman rank correlation over the thirty points is only $\boldsymbol{\rho=0.28}$: a link too weak to select models on.
In-domain rank is a poor compass for deployment --- model selection for an unknown target cannot read the in-domain leaderboard alone and must lean on the pooling lever of Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain-matrix}.
Chapter~5 sharpens this warning on the hybrid population, where the correlation even turns negative, and shows that the proposed architecture improves transfer by \emph{lifting} the whole trade-off rather than by riding it.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering

\caption{In-domain QWK versus mean QWK on the unseen-target cells, one point per trained
baseline configuration (circles: CMOSE-trained; triangles: DAiSEE-trained; colour:
architecture). The correlation is weak ($\rho=0.28$): in-domain strength barely predicts
transfer.}
\label{fig:indomain_vs_generalization_base}
\end{figure}

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:baseline-summary}

This chapter fixed the experimental frame --- protocol first, results after --- and characterised the monolithic baselines.
The evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) fixed \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} as the primary metrics --- each of the six metrics crowns a different winner, and QWK is the only metric bridging the otherwise orthogonal micro and macro blocks --- \textbf{CMOSE} as the development corpus (in-domain QWK 0.537 against DAiSEE's 0.166), and \textbf{cross-entropy} as the development loss (the QWK-maximising end of a genuine Pareto trade-off).
Under that protocol the strongest baseline is the OpenFace TCN at \textbf{QWK 0.537}, the bar the hybrid must beat (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}).
The prediction-level analysis (Section~\ref{sec:agreement}) showed that the fifteen configurations agree far less than their scores suggest ($\kappa=0.27$--$0.39$), that an oracle over them would reach 97.6\% against the best single model's 77.3\% --- headroom that naive voting cannot claim --- and that the OpenFace and I3D families fail on \emph{different} clips, the direct motivation for fusing them.
The cross-dataset study (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}) showed that baselines do not transfer off-the-shelf --- off-diagonal QWK collapses to near zero and the architecture ranking reshuffles, with I3D falling furthest --- and closed the two deferred arguments: accuracy stays deceptively high in exactly the cells where the ordinal signal disappears, and the loss ranking is the only one that survives the shift, vindicating both protocol choices; corpus pooling emerged as the most effective simple mitigation, and in-domain rank proved a weak predictor of transfer ($\rho=0.28$).
Chapter~5 now introduces the semantic-group hybrid and, under exactly this frame, asks whether learning to fuse the complementary streams beats these baselines in-domain and whether the gain compounds with pooling on unseen, real-world data.
