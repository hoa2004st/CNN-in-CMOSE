\section{Overview}
\label{sec:baseline-overview}

This chapter studies the \emph{monolithic baseline} models --- the standard practice of pushing a feature sequence through a single temporal encoder --- and in doing so fixes the methodological frame that the rest of the thesis depends on.
It has two jobs.
First, before anything is built, it settles \emph{what to optimise}, \emph{how to measure it}, and \emph{what bar a new architecture must clear}: these three decisions --- the training loss, the primary metrics, and the baseline QWK --- are read off the comparison of the five baselines under the three losses and govern every later experiment.
Second, it characterises how these off-the-shelf models behave, both \emph{in-domain} (trained and tested on the same corpus) and \emph{across datasets}, so that the proposed hybrid of Chapter~5 has a complete and honest reference point to improve on.
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results} compares the five baselines under the three losses and identifies the strongest one.
Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff} shows that the loss choice is a Pareto trade-off between headline accuracy and balanced ordinal performance, and from it selects the primary metrics and the development loss.
Section~\ref{sec:openface-vs-i3d} contrasts the two feature families the baselines are built on --- per-frame OpenFace descriptors and the clip-level I3D motion vector.
Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor} explains why the architecture study to follow is anchored on CMOSE.
Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} then takes the baselines out of domain with a 3$\times$3 cross-dataset matrix, establishing that engagement models do not transfer off-the-shelf and isolating the single most effective design lever for an unknown target.
Throughout, the class imbalance characterised in Section~\ref{sec:data} is the backdrop: it is the reason accuracy is reported but never trusted, and the reason the rare disengaged classes recur as the failure mode.

\section{Base Models, Losses, and Metrics (In-Domain)}
\label{sec:baseline-results}

This section settles the strongest baseline, the bar a new architecture must clear, and --- by contrasting just two metrics --- the first concrete reason accuracy cannot be that bar.
Five monolithic baselines --- four built on the OpenFace descriptor (an MLP, a TCN, an LSTM, and a Transformer) and one on the I3D clip vector (the I3D MLP) --- are trained and tested in-domain on CMOSE under each of the three losses.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{base_models_indomain.png}
\caption{Accuracy versus QWK for the five base models under the three losses, in-domain on
CMOSE: accuracy ranks the I3D MLP first while QWK ranks the OpenFace TCN first.}
\label{fig:base_models_indomain}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Accuracy and QWK disagree --- and accuracy is the one that misleads.}
Figure~\ref{fig:base_models_indomain} contrasts raw accuracy with QWK across the five baselines and three losses, and the two metrics do not even agree on which model wins.
Under cross-entropy the \textbf{I3D MLP} posts the highest \emph{accuracy} (0.773), whereas the \textbf{OpenFace TCN} posts the highest \emph{QWK} (0.537, against the I3D MLP's 0.519).
The reason is the class imbalance of Section~\ref{sec:data}: the I3D MLP buys accuracy by leaning on the majority \emph{Engage} class, a strategy that scores well on accuracy but is penalised by the chance-corrected, order-aware QWK.
Accuracy therefore rewards exactly the majority-collapse a useful monitor must avoid, which is the first concrete reason this thesis treats QWK --- not accuracy --- as the headline metric (the full metric choice is fixed in Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}).
The OpenFace TCN's local temporal bias gives it the best QWK of the grid, edging out the LSTM, the Transformer, and the flat MLP; this \textbf{QWK of 0.537 is the bar the hybrid must clear} in Chapter~5.
\input{Table/T2_base_indomain}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Accuracy and QWK rank the baselines differently, and accuracy is the metric inflated by the majority class --- the first reason to abandon it.
The strongest baseline on the order-aware QWK is the OpenFace TCN under cross-entropy at \textbf{QWK 0.537}, the reference the hybrid is measured against.
The next section shows how this ranking depends on the loss and from it fixes the three primary metrics and the development loss.

\section{The Loss--Metric Trade-off}
\label{sec:loss-tradeoff}

The ranking above was read under cross-entropy, but the loss is itself a design choice.
This section shows that the choice is a genuine Pareto trade-off --- no loss is best on every metric --- and uses that fact to fix the two remaining frame decisions: the primary metrics and the loss on which the architecture is developed.

\textbf{Each loss trades the metrics against each other.}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff} plots, for every baseline, how each primary metric moves as the loss is rebalanced from cross-entropy through weighted cross-entropy to the ordinal loss.
The pattern is consistent across models and is the central observation of the section: as the loss is rebalanced, \textbf{raw accuracy falls} while \textbf{macro-accuracy rises and macro-MAE drops (improves)} --- the balanced losses buy minority-class recall and a smaller balanced ordinal error at the cost of headline accuracy.
QWK, the chance-corrected agreement coefficient, is the most stable of the four and is generally highest under cross-entropy, because reweighting trades the agreement QWK measures for the per-class recall that macro-accuracy measures.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{loss_metric_tradeoff.png}
\caption{What each loss trades: the four primary metrics for every baseline across the
three losses, in-domain on CMOSE. Accuracy falls while macro-accuracy and macro-MAE
improve as the loss is rebalanced.}
\label{fig:loss_metric_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

The same trade-off can be read as a Pareto frontier in the two complementary balance metrics.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_accuracy_tradeoff} plots accuracy against macro-accuracy and Figure~\ref{fig:loss_pareto_mae} plots accuracy against macro-MAE; in both, the cross-entropy points sit toward high accuracy, and the weighted-CE and ordinal points move toward better balance.
For the I3D MLP, for instance, switching from cross-entropy to the ordinal loss moves the operating point from (accuracy 0.773, macro-accuracy 0.526) to (0.640, 0.635) and improves its macro-MAE from 0.585 to 0.458 --- the best balanced ordinal error of any baseline.
There is no universally best loss: the choice is a genuine Pareto trade-off, and for engagement monitoring --- where missing disengagement is costly --- the balanced losses are defensible despite their lower headline accuracy.

\textbf{Decision 1 --- the primary metrics.}
The trade-off above shows directly why accuracy cannot be the yardstick: a model can buy accuracy by collapsing onto \emph{Engage}, and on these imbalanced labels (Section~\ref{sec:data}) that collapse is rewarded rather than penalised.
The remainder of the thesis therefore reports \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE as the three primary metrics} --- QWK because it is corrected for chance, respects the class order, and cannot be inflated by majority prediction; macro-accuracy because it exposes minority-class recall directly; and \textbf{macro-MAE} (lower is better) because it measures \emph{how far} a prediction misses on the ordinal scale rather than only \emph{whether} it misses, averaged per class so that errors on the rare disengaged classes are not drowned out by the majority.
The three are complementary: QWK is a chance-corrected agreement coefficient, macro-accuracy a balanced recall, and macro-MAE a balanced ordinal distance, so a model is only judged strong when all three agree.
Raw accuracy and micro-averaged MAE are still reported in the tables for comparability with the literature, but they are not used to select models.

\textbf{Decision 2 --- the development loss.}
The strongest ordinal agreement of the whole baseline grid is obtained under \textbf{cross-entropy} (the bar-setting OpenFace TCN at QWK 0.537); the balanced losses trade QWK and accuracy for minority recall rather than improving the primary metric.
Cross-entropy is therefore the natural loss on which to develop the architecture: it maximises the primary metric and isolates the effect of the \emph{architecture} from the confound of the loss.
The semantic-group hybrid of Chapter~5 is consequently trained with cross-entropy, and the balanced losses are revisited where they matter most --- recovering rare-class recall and surviving distribution shift.
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce} shows representative training and validation loss curves; all baselines converge stably and early-stop well before the epoch cap, so the differences above are architectural rather than optimisation artefacts.

\begin{figure}[ht]

\caption{Representative training/validation loss curves (CMOSE, cross-entropy).}
\label{fig:loss_curves_cmose_ce}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Loss choice is a Pareto trade-off in which cross-entropy gives the best ordinal agreement while the balanced losses buy minority recall at the cost of accuracy.
The thesis therefore judges every model by \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} --- never accuracy alone --- and develops the architecture under \textbf{cross-entropy}, the loss that maximises the primary metric and isolates the effect of the architecture.

\section{OpenFace versus I3D Features}
\label{sec:openface-vs-i3d}

The baselines are built on two feature families of very different natures, and the contrast between them shapes the hybrid design that follows.
The \textbf{OpenFace} descriptor is a per-frame, structured account of facial behaviour --- gaze, eye and face landmarks, head pose, and action units --- so an OpenFace baseline sees the \emph{temporal dynamics} of the face and is encoded by a sequence model (here the TCN, LSTM, or Transformer).
The \textbf{I3D} feature is a single clip-level vector summarising global appearance and motion; the I3D baseline is therefore an MLP over one pooled vector, with no per-frame temporal structure to exploit.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{openface_vs_i3d.png}
\caption{Best OpenFace encoder (the TCN) versus the I3D MLP on the three primary metrics,
in-domain on CMOSE under cross-entropy: OpenFace wins all three.}
\label{fig:openface_vs_i3d}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:openface_vs_i3d} contrasts the strongest OpenFace baseline (the TCN) with the I3D MLP on the three primary metrics under cross-entropy.
The OpenFace TCN wins on \emph{all three}: QWK (0.537 versus 0.519), macro-accuracy (0.535 versus 0.526), and macro-MAE (0.547 versus 0.585, lower is better).
The fine-grained, per-frame facial dynamics it encodes carry the cues --- a head turning away, gaze drifting off-screen --- that separate the rarer disengaged levels, which the single pooled I3D vector blurs.
Yet the two families are \textbf{complementary rather than redundant}.
The I3D MLP's strength lies elsewhere: it tops \emph{raw accuracy} through its alignment with the majority \emph{Engage} class (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}), and it is the most \emph{reweightable} baseline, recovering the best balanced ordinal error of any model under the ordinal loss (macro-MAE 0.458, Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}), whereas the OpenFace encoders gain less from reweighting.
Global appearance/motion and fine ordinal facial structure are thus captured best by different families --- the direct motivation for \emph{fusing} the two streams in the hybrid of Chapter~5 rather than choosing between them.

\section{Anchoring Development on CMOSE}
\label{sec:cmose-anchor}

The comparisons above, and the architecture study of Chapter~5, are conducted on CMOSE rather than DAiSEE for a principled reason: DAiSEE carries far weaker in-domain ordinal signal.
Table~\ref{tab:T7_indomain_datasets} and Figure~\ref{fig:indomain_cmose_vs_daisee} show that the best in-domain model reaches \textbf{QWK 0.537 on CMOSE but only 0.166 on DAiSEE} (macro-accuracy 0.535 versus 0.289, and macro-MAE 0.547 versus 1.226 --- more than twice the ordinal error).
On DAiSEE the best models barely exceed chance-level ordinal agreement, so architecture differences there would be dominated by label noise rather than model quality.
CMOSE is the only corpus on which architecture choices can be compared meaningfully; DAiSEE re-enters the study as a \emph{transfer} source and target in the cross-dataset matrix below and in Chapter~5, not as a development corpus.

\section{Cross-Dataset Generalization of the Baselines}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

In-domain numbers are not the deployment question.
This section takes the baselines out of domain --- training on one corpus and testing on another --- to ask whether any of the in-domain ordinal signal survives a change of corpus, using the chance-corrected metrics fixed in Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff} rather than accuracy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{crossdomain_base.png}
\caption{Cross-domain generalisation, best base model per cell, on the three primary
metrics: QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE (lower is better).}
\label{fig:crossdomain_base}
\end{figure}

The 3$\times$3 heatmaps (Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain_base} and Table~\ref{tab:T3_crossdomain}) are the central baseline generalisation result.
\textbf{On the diagonal} (train and test on the same corpus) QWK is healthy (CMOSE 0.54), macro-accuracy is well above chance (CMOSE 0.64), and ordinal error is low (CMOSE macro-MAE 0.46).
\textbf{Off the diagonal all three collapse together}: CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE QWK $=0.02$ and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE $=0.05$ (essentially chance-level agreement); macro-accuracy falls toward the 0.25 chance floor (DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.29); and macro-MAE inflates toward a full class (CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE 0.77, DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.99) even for the best models, against $\approx0.46$ on the diagonal.
Crucially, \textbf{raw accuracy hides this collapse}: in Table~\ref{tab:T3_crossdomain} the CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE accuracy is a deceptively reasonable 0.49 and DAiSEE$\rightarrow$CMOSE 0.69, purely because predicting the majority class scores well on imbalanced targets --- which is exactly why accuracy is omitted from the heatmaps and the three order- and balance-aware primaries are shown instead.
This is direct, quantitative vindication of the metric choice made in Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}, and shows engagement models are \textbf{not portable off-the-shelf}.
\input{Table/T3_crossdomain}

\textbf{Reading the matrix.}
The gap between accuracy and QWK is itself the diagnostic.
On the CMOSE$\rightarrow$DAiSEE cell accuracy stays near 0.49 while QWK falls to 0.02: the model has not learned a transferable ordinal scale, it has merely inherited a class prior that happens to overlap the target's majority.
A single accuracy number would have reported a partial success; the chance-corrected, order-aware QWK reports the failure that is actually there.
The collapse is, moreover, roughly symmetric --- neither public corpus transfers to the other (0.02 and 0.05) --- which rules out the simpler reading that one corpus is a superset of the other and points instead to a genuine shift in both label priors and recording conditions.

\textbf{The lever: pool the source corpora.}
The most actionable cell is the unseen \textbf{Private} column: training on the \textbf{Combined} corpus gives the best private-set QWK (0.285), beating DAiSEE-only (0.256) and CMOSE-only (0.190).
Pooling source datasets is a simple, effective mitigation for an unknown target distribution: the union of CMOSE and DAiSEE spans a wider range of label priors than either alone, so an unseen target sits closer to the pooled distribution.
This is the single most effective design lever for an unknown target, and it is carried into Chapter~5, where it is combined with the architecture contribution on the decisive private set (\textbf{RQ4}).

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Engagement classifiers built on the standard monolithic recipe do not generalise across datasets: off-diagonal QWK falls to near zero even when accuracy looks acceptable, so ordinal agreement --- not accuracy --- must be reported on imbalanced engagement data.
Among the available levers, \emph{pooling source corpora} is the most effective simple remedy for an unknown target.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:baseline-summary}

This chapter fixed the experimental frame and characterised the monolithic baselines.
The in-domain comparison (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-results}) established that the strongest baseline is the OpenFace TCN at \textbf{QWK 0.537}, the bar the hybrid must beat.
The loss study (Section~\ref{sec:loss-tradeoff}) showed that loss choice is a Pareto trade-off in which cross-entropy gives the best ordinal agreement; from it the thesis selected \textbf{QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE} --- not accuracy --- as the primary metrics and \textbf{cross-entropy} as the development loss.
The feature comparison (Section~\ref{sec:openface-vs-i3d}) showed that OpenFace and I3D are complementary --- I3D wins raw accuracy, OpenFace wins the order-aware metrics --- motivating their fusion in the hybrid.
CMOSE was identified as the only corpus with enough in-domain signal to separate architectures (Section~\ref{sec:cmose-anchor}), and the cross-dataset study (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}) showed that baselines do not transfer off-the-shelf --- off-diagonal QWK collapses while accuracy hides it --- with corpus pooling the most effective simple mitigation.
Chapter~5 now introduces the semantic-group hybrid and, under exactly this frame, asks whether decomposing the OpenFace descriptor beats these baselines in-domain and whether the gain compounds with pooling on unseen, real-world data.
