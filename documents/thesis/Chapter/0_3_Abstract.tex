\begin{center}
    \Large{\textbf{ABSTRACT}}\\
\end{center}
\vspace{1cm}

Reading student engagement from video automatically helps online and hybrid classes, yet it is hard: the labels are ordinal (Highly-Disengage, Disengage, Engage, Highly-Engage), badly imbalanced, and subjective, and models are rarely tested on a dataset other than the one they were trained on.
This thesis studies how to classify engagement from the features OpenFace extracts frame by frame and the features I3D extracts from the whole clip at once. It makes three contributions: a hybrid model that splits the facial features into meaningful groups, a private test set I collected myself, and a generalisation study conducted across several datasets.
First, I compare the baseline models and choose the evaluation methods. The six metrics clearly disagree on the best model, and the cross-dataset study later shows the shortcoming of accuracy. For this reason, quadratic-weighted kappa (QWK) is the single metric used to select the best model, with macro-accuracy and macro-MAE kept as secondary checks. The loss is shown to trade QWK against those checks, so cross-entropy, which gives the highest QWK, is used for development. Looking at errors clip by clip also shows that the OpenFace and I3D models miss on different clips, which is why they are combined.

First, the thesis proposes a feature-group hybrid.
Instead of feeding all 709 OpenFace features into one model, the features are split into five meaningful groups (gaze, eye landmarks, face landmarks, head pose, and action units), and each group is given its own temporal encoder (a Temporal Convolutional Network, a Transformer, or an LSTM), optionally combined with an I3D motion stream and trained with a small head on each stream.
Trying all 243 ways to assign encoders to groups, with and without I3D, shows that the I3D hybrid family scores higher than the best single-encoder baseline on CMOSE (median QWK 0.553 against 0.537; best 0.605), that the strongest models mix encoder types, and that the only group with a clear encoder preference is head pose, which prefers a TCN.

Second, it introduces a private test set I collected and hand-labelled: webcam clips made with the same OpenFace/I3D pipeline as the public datasets and kept only for testing. It tests how well a model works on real students recorded on their own webcams, where the camera angles and conditions differ from the public datasets.

Third, it runs a 3$\times$3 cross-dataset study (training on CMOSE, DAiSEE, or both together; testing on each public test set plus the private set) and treats the private set as the deciding real-world test.
Engagement models do not transfer across datasets: off-diagonal QWK drops to near zero even when plain accuracy looks fine. On the unseen private set the three contributions add up: training on both datasets together with the hybrid gives the best private-set result (QWK 0.379 against 0.285 for the best baseline, a bigger gap than in-domain).
The thesis argues that quadratic-weighted kappa, not plain accuracy, must be the measure that selects models when engagement labels are imbalanced, with macro-accuracy and macro-MAE reported only as secondary checks.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} student engagement recognition, affective computing, OpenFace, I3D, temporal convolutional networks, ordinal classification, class imbalance, cross-dataset generalisation.
