\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Online and hybrid classes are now well integrated into public education, where lessons run through video calls and the webcam is the main connection between a student and a teacher, as well as the main evidence of whether students are following the lesson or not. In a physical classroom a teacher observes students' expressions, postures, and drifting attention, and adapts the lesson accordingly. Yet, a teacher who faces dozens of video tiles at once hardly receives this feedback and can no longer tell when a learner has stopped attending. 

The problem this thesis addresses is the automatic recognition of student engagement, which determines from the webcam view of a student how engaged that student is with the lesson. This would let a teacher see in real time which students are losing focus and let a platform adjust the pace of a lesson, and aggregated over a session or a cohort it would give an objective account of how well the material holds attention.

\section{Background and Problems of Research}
\label{sec:background}

Vision-based engagement recognition estimates a learner's engagement from a video of that learner. Most work casts it as a classification task, in which a short webcam clip of one student is assigned one of four ordinal engagement levels ranging from highly disengaged to highly engaged. Two main approaches have been pursued. The first encodes each frame with a facial-behaviour toolkit, most often OpenFace~\cite{openface2}, which estimates gaze direction, head pose, facial landmarks, and action-unit intensities. The resulting feature sequence is then passed to a classical classifier or a recurrent network, as in an OpenFace$+$BiLSTM pipeline~\cite{openface_bilstm} or in classical models over summary features~\cite{classical_ml_engagement}. The second approach learns directly from pixels with deep video models, for instance an EfficientNetV2 backbone followed by an LSTM~\cite{efficientnet_lstm_engagement}, or it adapts context-modelling ideas from affect recognition~\cite{context_aware_emotion_3d}. Progress is measured on a small number of datasets recorded in the wild. Two of these are DAiSEE~\cite{daisee}, a widely used public dataset that provides raw video, and CMOSE~\cite{cmose}, a more recent and larger dataset that is labelled by experts and ships precomputed OpenFace and I3D features. A systematic review~\cite{review_engagement_cv} finds severe class imbalance and subjective labels to be common and unsolved problems across the field.

Two limitations of this body of work motivate the thesis. The first is that the facial features are passed through a single encoder. In CMOSE a single OpenFace frame is a 709-dimensional vector, and this vector bundles measurements of very different physical kinds. It contains a few gaze values, hundreds of eye- and face-landmark coordinates, a set of head-pose parameters, and a set of action-unit strengths. These parts also move at different speeds. The eyes flick quickly and noisily, the head turns slowly and smoothly, and the action units fire in short bursts. Joining all 709 numbers and passing them through one sequence encoder forces a single temporal model onto signals that do not share a timescale, and it obscures the contribution of each behavioural channel.

The second limitation is that studies rely on accuracy as their primary metric and lack a reliable evaluation of generalisation. Almost every study reports accuracy in-domain, training and testing on the same dataset, yet the only deployment that matters uses data recorded with different cameras and conditions. The classes are also heavily imbalanced toward the attentive levels, so a model that simply predicts the majority class can reach a high accuracy while learning nothing that transfers. The metric most papers report can therefore hide the very failure it should expose. Distribution shift makes this worse. Facial features are read from the camera's view of the face, so a change of camera angle between training and deployment moves the features even when the underlying engagement is unchanged, and this kind of transfer is rarely measured.

These two limitations define the gap this thesis targets. The mixed-feature limitation calls for a representation that preserves the structure of the facial features instead of flattening it. The generalisation limitation calls for an evaluation that measures transfer directly, using metrics that majority prediction cannot inflate.

\section{Research Objectives and Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is twofold. The first aim is to improve the recognition of engagement on the four-level ordinal scale beyond what standard single-encoder models achieve. The second aim is to give an accurate account of the problem, above all its strong class imbalance and its behaviour when the data changes. To meet these aims, this thesis proposes a solution direction for each of the two problems identified in Section~\ref{sec:background}, one that concerns how the features are modelled and one that concerns how a model is evaluated.

The first direction of work concerns how the features are modelled, and it preserves the structure of the facial descriptor instead of flattening it into a single block. This responds to the limitation that heterogeneous facial channels are forced through one encoder. The OpenFace descriptor is split into five groups that correspond to distinct facial subsystems, namely gaze, eye landmarks, face landmarks, head pose, and action units, and each group is encoded on its own by an encoder suited to how that subsystem moves in time. A global I3D motion stream can be added alongside the five groups. The resulting embeddings are then joined and classified together, and every stream also carries its own small head so that each channel stays useful on its own. This proposal is the feature-group hybrid model developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:method} (Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid}), and it is the main model this thesis puts forward against the single-encoder baselines. Because it is not obvious in advance which encoder best suits each group, or whether the added motion stream helps, this thesis examines these choices across the full design space of the model rather than fixing them by hand.

The second direction of work concerns how a model is evaluated, and it applies an evaluation designed for imbalanced ordinal labels and for deployment. This responds to the limitation that accuracy is used as the primary metric while generalisation is left untested. Every model is selected and ranked on quadratic-weighted kappa (QWK), an ordinal-agreement metric that majority prediction cannot inflate, while balanced accuracy and balanced ordinal error are reported only as secondary checks. Generalisation is then measured directly rather than assumed. Every model is trained and tested across three datasets in a cross-dataset protocol, and one of these datasets was collected for this thesis to present recording conditions that the public data never shows. Together these choices form the evaluation framework under which the proposed model and the baselines are compared.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item This thesis proposes a hybrid model that splits the 709 OpenFace features into five groups. Each group is encoded by its own TCN, Transformer, or LSTM, a global I3D motion stream is optionally added, and every stream is trained with an auxiliary head (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). All 243 encoder assignments are tried, with the I3D stream enabled and disabled. The configurations that enable the I3D stream score higher than the strongest baseline, with a median QWK of 0.553 against 0.537 and a best of 0.605. The gain belongs to the whole family rather than to one configuration, and head pose is the only group with a clear encoder preference, namely a TCN, which holds under distribution shift (Section~\ref{sec:cmose-indomain}).

  \item A test-only dataset of 366 webcam clips was collected for this thesis. The clips were cut to a fixed length, filtered for tracking quality, labelled by hand, and processed with the same OpenFace and I3D pipeline as the public datasets. This dataset measures performance on real students recorded on their own webcams, where camera angles and conditions differ from the public datasets (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

  \item This thesis runs a 3$\times$3 cross-dataset study, training on CMOSE, DAiSEE, or their combination, and testing on each public test set together with the private set. The study shows that engagement models do not transfer to a new dataset, that accuracy hides this collapse while QWK exposes it, and that combined training is the simplest effective fix. On the private set the architecture and the combined training reinforce each other. The proposed hybrid model trained on both datasets is the best model, at QWK 0.379 against 0.285 for the best baseline, a wider gap than in-domain, which shows the value of the three contributions together (Sections~\ref{sec:cross-dataset} and~\ref{sec:private-result}).
\end{enumerate}

A reproducible pipeline turns about 1500 trained runs into the figures and tables of this thesis. It runs from feature extraction through training and cross-dataset evaluation, and on to the generation of every figure and table.

\section{Organization of Thesis}
\label{sec:organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

Chapter~\ref{chap:litreview} reviews vision-based engagement recognition and the background the thesis relies on. It sets the scope and compares the feature-based and end-to-end approaches together with their limits. It then presents the background knowledge that later chapters assume. This background covers the OpenFace and I3D feature extractors, the three temporal architecture families of TCN, LSTM, and Transformer, the loss functions for imbalanced ordinal labels, the ordinal metrics, and the statistical measures used to analyse the results.

Chapter~\ref{chap:method} presents the methodology. It gives an overview of the pipeline with diagrams of the baseline and the proposed model. It then describes the three training datasets and their imbalance, the private test set, and the leak-free preprocessing. Finally it defines the single-encoder baselines and builds the feature-group decomposition and the proposed hybrid model on top of them.

Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports the experimental results, centred on the proposed hybrid model. It sets out the loss forms, the training regime, and the 3$\times$3 cross-dataset evaluation matrix. It confirms that the evaluation protocol holds across the model's 486 configurations, and it answers the design questions of which encoder suits which group and whether the I3D stream helps. It then takes the model out of domain, where it outperforms the single-encoder baseline in every cell of the matrix and, with combined training, reaches the best result on the private set.

Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} draws the findings together, restates the contributions in the light of the evidence, discusses the limits, and proposes future work. The future work includes targeting the rarest disengaged class, applying explicit domain adaptation, and learning the group encoders rather than selecting them by hand.

Appendix~\ref{chap:baseline} presents the supporting single-encoder baseline study in a protocol-first order. It establishes the metrics of QWK, macro-accuracy, and macro-MAE, the development dataset of CMOSE, and the development loss of cross-entropy. It then reads the in-domain leaderboard and studies prediction agreement to show that the OpenFace and I3D families make different errors. Finally it takes the baselines out of domain in the 3$\times$3 matrix, which sets the reference level against which the proposed model is measured.
